\begin{abstract}
Prompts for in-context learning (ICL) describe a task, demonstrate it through examples, or both. Yet how descriptions work, especially in relation to demonstrations, remains unclear. Through the Bayesian view of ICL, we model descriptions as informative priors, characterized by their \emph{specificity} and \emph{reliability}. We adapt the effective sample size (ESS) from Bayesian statistics to quantify a description's predictive value as an equivalent number of demonstrations. Our theoretical results on in-context linear regression tasks show that a reliable description's ESS grows with its specificity and with the task's dimension. However, the ESS falls sharply if the description may be irrelevant: in a 16-dimensional task with no demonstrations, a one-in-ten chance of irrelevance cuts a precise description's ESS from 117 to 13.5. We then test whether Transformer networks and pretrained language models, including frontier models, attain these values. Meta-trained Transformers come close to the Bayes-optimal ESS and adapt to unreliable descriptions when trained on them. Pretrained language models, however, place too little weight on precise descriptions when answering without reasoning, and closely match the Bayesian reference only when reasoning is enabled. Taken together, these results extend the Bayesian analysis of ICL from demonstrations to descriptions.
\end{abstract}

Large language models (LLMs) are capable of in-context learning (ICL): given a prompt that specifies a task through a natural-language description, demonstrations, or both, they can predict the output for a new query input \citep{brown2020}. In practice, prompts consist mostly of system instructions, user instructions, skill files, and earlier conversation turns, which typically describe the task rather than demonstrate it \citep{mao2025,patsakis2026}. The balance shifts further toward description as LLMs carry out increasingly long-horizon tasks \citep{kwa2025}, for which demonstrations are costly to provide.

Theories of ICL, however, have mostly focused on prompts consisting of demonstrations alone \citep{xie2022,garg2022,akyurek2023}. The few that include descriptions study only specific types, such as a descriptor of the input distribution \citep{huangge2025}, a set of candidate hypotheses \citep{lin2025}, or related datasets supplied as context \citep{zhu2026}; none offers a systematic treatment of descriptions. We therefore ask: how can we characterize natural-language descriptions in general, so as to understand and analyze them formally? And how well do learners, whether meta-trained Transformers or pretrained language models, use descriptions in practice?

We study the first question through the Bayesian view of ICL, in which a model infers a latent task from the demonstrations \citep{xie2022}; a description can then be modeled as a prior over tasks. Descriptions differ in how much they say about a task: ``write this report as an accountant'' leaves most of the task open, whereas a step-by-step workflow leaves little. They also differ in whether they are right: an outdated instruction, for example, is simply wrong. We formalize these two properties in $d$-dimensional Gaussian linear regression, a standard controlled setting for ICL \citep{garg2022,panwar2024}. Without a description, the coefficients follow a Gaussian baseline prior with variance $a_0$. With probability $p$, a description is relevant, and the coefficients are Gaussian around its center with precision $c$; otherwise, the description is wrong, and we model it as independent of the task. We thus characterize a description by two quantities: its \emph{specificity}, set by $c$, and its \emph{reliability}, the prior probability $p$ that it is relevant.

To compare descriptions with demonstrations, we adapt measures that express a prior's impact, or a predictor's performance, as a number of observations \citep{wiesenfarth2020,reimherr2021,lescao2021,selby2025}. A description's \emph{predictive effective sample size} (ESS) is the number of additional demonstrations that a learner needs, without the description, to reach the same expected error. We compute ESS for the Bayes-optimal predictor and for trained models. The first gives the information that a description carries under the statistical model; the second gives the benefit that a learner realizes. For the second question, we test how close trained learners come to the Bayes-optimal ESS.

Our main findings are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Precision and dimension determine the value of reliable descriptions.} With precision $c$ fixed at a positive integer, zero-demonstration ESS approaches $c+d+1$ as the baseline prior becomes diffuse (Section~\ref{sec:specificity}).
\item \textbf{Uncertain relevance limits zero-demonstration ESS.} Any fixed positive probability of irrelevance imposes a finite ceiling as precision grows; with demonstrations, a learner can also infer whether the description is relevant (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}).
\item \textbf{For Transformers, description use depends on training; for language models, on reasoning.} Transformers trained from scratch on the Gaussian model must learn a description's precision and reliability from their training data. They approach the Bayes-optimal ESS, and the weight they give a description follows the reliability of that data. Pretrained language models are instead tested on numerical mean-estimation tasks whose prompts state both properties; for most models tested, enabling reasoning brings their use of the stated precision close to the Bayesian reference (Sections~\ref{sec:networks}--\ref{sec:llm}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Framework}\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Tasks and demonstrations.}
A task is a vector $w\in\R^d$ with baseline prior $\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d)$, where $a_0>0$. A prompt contains $n$ demonstrations $D_n=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a query input $x$, with
\[
x_i,x\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\qquad
y_i=w^\top x_i+\epsilon_i,\quad
\epsilon_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]
Inputs and observation noise are independent of the task and one another. Numerical illustrations in Sections~\ref{sec:specificity}--\ref{sec:reliability} use $d=16$ and $a_0=10$; other dimensions and baseline variances give the same pattern (Table~\ref{tab:grid}).

\paragraph{Descriptions and relevance.}
A description supplies a center $m\in\R^d$. The description's \emph{specificity} is represented by its precision $c$, which is at least the baseline prior's precision $a_0^{-1}$; its \emph{reliability} is $p\in[0,1]$. Let $r=(ca_0)^{-1}\in(0,1]$ be the fraction of baseline variance remaining in the relevant component. With a latent relevance indicator $Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ independent of $m$, the joint model is
\begin{align*}
m&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(1-r)a_0I_d),\\
w\mid m,Z=1&\sim\mathcal{N}(m,ra_0I_d),\\
w\mid m,Z=0&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d).
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
w\mid m\sim p\,\mathcal{N}(m,c^{-1}I_d)
 +(1-p)\,\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d).
\]
This two-component structure is the robust mixture prior that Bayesian clinical trials use to borrow external information that may not apply \citep{schmidli2014}. The irrelevant component represents a description that is simply wrong: such a description says nothing about the task. A description that is merely inaccurate is a less precise one. Integrating over $m$ recovers $w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d)$ under either component, so comparisons with and without descriptions concern the same task population.

A description is \emph{relevant} when $Z=1$ and \emph{irrelevant} when $Z=0$. We use \emph{reliable} for $p=1$ and \emph{unreliable} for $p<1$.

\paragraph{Prediction error.}
For a prediction $\hat y$, we measure expected squared error against the noise-free query response:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regret}
R=\E\big[(\hat y-w^\top x)^2\big].
\end{equation}
We call this quantity \emph{prediction error}, or \emph{risk}; it excludes irreducible query noise. The expectation is over tasks, descriptions, demonstrations, and queries. For a fixed learner, $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)$ and $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)$ denote risk with and without a description, and $R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)$ denotes the reliable case. As a function of the demonstration count, $R^{\mathrm{demo}}$ is the learner's \emph{baseline curve}.

\begin{definition}[Predictive effective sample size]\label{def:ess}
Given $n$ demonstrations, let $n^*$ be the smallest $t\geq0$ satisfying $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)=R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)$. When this crossing exists, the description's ESS is
\[
\ess_n=n^*-n.
\]
Noninteger counts refer to an interpolated baseline curve.
\end{definition}

An ESS of $10$ means that $n$ demonstrations plus the description match the error of $n+10$ demonstrations alone. A learner without a description still has prior knowledge, from the baseline prior or from training, and ESS measures what the description adds to it. ESS is therefore measured against each learner's own baseline curve. A large ESS thus need not imply low absolute error, and a negative ESS means that the description raises error.

We match error because it can be measured for any learner. The standard ESS of a prior instead matches the prior's information to that of a sample, and computing it requires the prior in explicit form \citep{morita2008}. A network or a language model holds its prior knowledge only implicitly, in its weights.

\section{The Value of a Reliable Description}\label{sec:specificity}

For $p=1$, a description's value without demonstrations depends on task dimension as well as precision. Write $W_n=X_n^\top X_n$, where the rows of $X_n$ are the demonstration inputs. Gaussian conjugacy gives the Bayes-optimal risks
\begin{align*}
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)&=\E\operatorname{tr}(a_0^{-1}I_d+W_n)^{-1},\\
R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)&=\E\operatorname{tr}(cI_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\end{align*}
We state the result for a diffuse baseline prior, the limit $a_0\to\infty$, in which a learner knows little about the task without the description.

\begin{proposition}[Reliable descriptions]\label{prop:reliable}
Let $p=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item With $r\in(0,1]$ fixed, $\ess_0\geq(1-r)d$, and $\ess_0\to(1-r)d$ as $a_0\to\infty$.
\item With positive integer $c$ fixed, $\ess_0\to c+d+1$ as $a_0\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Part (i) fixes the fraction $1-r$ of baseline variance that the description removes, so the description's precision $c=(ra_0)^{-1}$ decreases as $a_0$ grows. With fewer than $d$ demonstrations, the inputs leave some directions unconstrained, and as $a_0\to\infty$ the error in those directions dominates. In the limit, the description then replaces $(1-r)d$ demonstrations; at finite $a_0$, it replaces at least that many (Appendix~\ref{app:specificity}).

Part (ii) fixes the precision instead. The result follows by comparing the description's risk $d/c$ with the limiting baseline risk, which is $d/(n-d-1)$ for integer $n>d+1$ \citep{muirhead1982}: the two match at $n=c+d+1$. For noninteger $c>1$, the limit lies between the limits for the two neighboring integers. If every direction received its expected information, $\E W_n=nI_d$, the baseline risk would be $d/n$ and the description would be worth $c$ demonstrations. Random inputs constrain some directions less than that, so the risk is $d/(n-d-1)$, and matching the description takes $d+1$ more demonstrations.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/ess_sq.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Without demonstrations, reliability limits the benefit of specificity.} Bayes-optimal $\ess_0$ versus precision $c$, for $p=1$ and $p=0.9$, with $d=16$ and $a_0=10$. The dotted line is the finite ceiling as $c\to\infty$ at $p=0.9$. Estimates use eight batches of $4{,}000$ prompts; standard errors are below $0.02$ demonstrations.}
\label{fig:ess}

At $d=16$ and $a_0=10$, a reliable description with $c=1$ has ESS $15.6$, compared with the lower bound $(1-r)d=14.4$. Increasing $c$ to $100$ raises ESS to approximately $117$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ess}). With many demonstrations, the dimension-dependent contribution vanishes: $\ess_n\to c-a_0^{-1}$. This limit, the precision that the description adds to the baseline prior, is the description's conventional prior ESS (Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}(ii) below). After $100$ demonstrations, the $c=1$ description has ESS $1.1$, close to its added precision of $0.9$. A description of the whole task thus loses value as demonstrations accumulate. One that specifies only part of the task, constraining some directions of $w$ and not others, can instead gain value (Appendix~\ref{app:partial}).

\section{The Cost of Unreliability}\label{sec:reliability}

We now allow the description to be irrelevant with probability $1-p$. The Bayes-optimal predictor must then weigh the two components by each one's probability given the demonstrations.

For $0<p<1$, let $\mu_z=\E[w\mid m,D_n,Z=z]$ be the component posterior means and let $\pi_n=P(Z=1\mid m,D_n)$ be the \emph{posterior relevance probability}. Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999} gives
\[
\hat y=\big[\pi_n\mu_1+(1-\pi_n)\mu_0\big]^\top x.
\]
The law of total variance then yields the following risk decomposition.

\begin{proposition}[Risk under uncertain relevance]\label{prop:unreliable}
For every $n\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
\begin{split}
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)={}&pR^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)\\
&+(1-p)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)+\mathcal{V}_n,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where
\[
\mathcal{V}_n=\E\!\left[\pi_n(1-\pi_n)
\big((\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top x\big)^2\right]\geq0.
\]
Without demonstrations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zero}
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(0)=\big[1-p^2(1-r)\big]a_0d.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Here $\mathcal{V}_n$ is the expected between-component posterior variance. The first two terms in Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp} are the risk of the Bayes-optimal predictor that also observes $Z$, so $\mathcal{V}_n$ is the excess Bayes risk due to unobserved relevance. At $n=0$, a predictor that observed $Z$ would remove a fraction $p(1-r)$ of baseline risk. The Bayes-optimal predictor does not observe $Z$: it shrinks $m$ by the factor $p$ and removes only $p^2(1-r)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:reliability}).

\begin{corollary}[ESS under uncertain relevance]\label{cor:ess}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item For $0<p<1$, define $n_p^{\sharp}$ by
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n_p^{\sharp})=(1-p^2)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0).
\]
Then $\ess_0<n_p^{\sharp}$ at every finite $c$, and $\ess_0\to n_p^{\sharp}$ as $c\to\infty$. As $a_0\to\infty$ with $r$ fixed, $\ess_0\to p^2(1-r)d$ and $n_p^{\sharp}\to p^2d$. For $p=0$, both ESS and the ceiling are zero.
\item With $d$, $a_0$, and $c$ fixed, as $n\to\infty$,
\[
\begin{cases}
\ess_n\to c-a_0^{-1},&p=1,\\
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\ess_n\leq p(c-a_0^{-1}),&p<1.
\end{cases}
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Without demonstrations, uncertain relevance imposes an error floor.}
For fixed $p<1$, the risk in Equation~\eqref{eq:zero} approaches the positive floor $(1-p^2)a_0d$ as $c\to\infty$, giving a finite ceiling on zero-demonstration ESS. We take $p=0.9$, the weight on the informative component in the robust-prior example of \citet{schmidli2014}. With $d=16$ and $a_0=10$, increasing $c$ from $1$ to $100$ then raises ESS only from $12.0$ to $13.5$, compared with $15.6$ to $117$ at $p=1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ess}). The $c=100$ description nearly reaches the ceiling $n_p^{\sharp}$, which is approximately $13.5$ at $a_0=10$. As the baseline prior becomes diffuse, the ceiling tends to $p^2d=12.96$.

\paragraph{Demonstrations distinguish low precision from uncertain relevance.}
Without demonstrations, an unreliable description has the same risk as a reliable description that leaves a fraction $1-p^2(1-r)$ of the baseline variance. For example, a $c=100$ description with $p=0.9$ and a reliable one with $c\approx0.52$ both have ESS about $13.5$ here. After $100$ demonstrations, their ESS values are $86.6$ and $0.5$; the first is near the bound $p(c-a_0^{-1})\approx89.9$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}(ii). Demonstrations can provide evidence for the high-precision component of the unreliable description; the reliable low-precision description has no such component.

\section{Meta-Trained Transformers}\label{sec:networks}

Transformers meta-trained on demonstrations can approach Bayes-optimal prediction \citep{garg2022,genewein2025}. We test whether they also attain the Bayes-optimal ESS of a description. Following \citet{garg2022}, we train from scratch on the model of Section~\ref{sec:setting}, with $a_0=10$ and $d=5$, the dimension used by \citet{huangge2025}. A prompt contains an optional description token carrying $m$, up to $20$ demonstration tokens, and a query token \citep{huangge2025}. The description token does not state $c$ or $p$; a network must learn these properties across training tasks. Half the prompts omit the description; a network's predictions on such prompts give its own baseline curve. We train separate networks for $c\in\{1,10\}$ and $p\in\{1,0.99,0.9\}$, with two or three seeds per configuration (Appendix~\ref{app:networks}). The two precisions are a factor of ten apart, and so are the two nonzero probabilities of irrelevance, $1-p=0.01$ and $1-p=0.1$. We omit $c=100$: such a description is worth more demonstrations than a $20$-demonstration prompt can show.

\paragraph{Matched training and test distributions.}
At $n=0$, networks trained with $c=10$ and $p=1$ achieve mean ESS $14.3$. The Bayes-optimal ESS is $16.1$ on the same prompts (Figure~\ref{fig:networks}a) and $15.9$ on a larger sample (Table~\ref{tab:networks}). For networks trained at lower reliability or with $c=1$, ESS is within $0.3$ of the Bayes-optimal value. The networks also show the dependence on dimension: their ESS rises from $14.3$ at $d=5$ to $17.8$ at $d=10$ (Appendix~\ref{app:networks}).

\paragraph{Reliability shifts.}
The networks learn reliability from their training data, but descriptions at test time may be more or less reliable. We therefore evaluate each network at test reliabilities that differ from its training reliability, and compare it with two Bayesian predictors \citep{raventos2023}: one that uses the training reliability $p_{\mathrm{train}}$, and one that uses the test reliability $p_{\mathrm{test}}$ and is therefore Bayes-optimal. At $n=0$, a Bayesian predictor with reliability $p$ predicts $p\,m^\top x$. We therefore summarize a network's predictions by the \emph{fitted description weight} $q$, the coefficient from regressing them on $m^\top x$.

The networks keep the reliability they were trained with, because $m$ alone does not reveal that the test reliability has changed. For $c=10$, networks trained at $p=0.9$ retain weights near $0.9$ even when every test description is relevant (Table~\ref{tab:networks}), and so realize only part of the Bayes-optimal ESS (Figure~\ref{fig:networks}b). The opposite shift, from reliable descriptions in training to unreliable ones at test time, costs little without demonstrations (Figure~\ref{fig:networks}b) but makes the description harmful once six or more demonstrations are observed. Networks trained at $p=0.99$ avoid this harm (Appendices~\ref{app:misspecification} and~\ref{app:networks}).

\section{Pretrained Language Models}\label{sec:llm}

Unlike the meta-trained networks, pretrained language models must be told a description's precision and reliability. We ask whether they use what the prompt states. To isolate the use of a description from the ability to carry out linear regression in context, we use numerical mean estimation. It is the scalar case of our model with fixed input $x=1$, and it has an exact Bayesian reference. Using a stated precision requires weighting the description and the demonstrations by their precisions. Reasoning may help a model carry out this computation, so we compare models with reasoning enabled and disabled.

\subsection{Task and Evaluation}

Each numerical task has a hidden mean $w\sim\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$. Its demonstrations are drawn from $\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^2)$, with $\sigma=60$, and rounded to integers. Language models predict short unsigned numbers best \citep{gruver2023,requeima2024}, so a task is redrawn if any of its numbers falls outside $100$ to $999$. A description gives a center $m$ and a standard deviation $\tau$: “Its mean was drawn from a normal distribution with mean 510 and standard deviation 30.” Before rounding and redrawing, a relevant description satisfies $w\mid m\sim\mathcal{N}(m,\tau^2)$. We measure its precision relative to one observation, $c=\sigma^2/\tau^2$, retaining the unit-noise convention of the theory. Stated standard deviations $60$, $30$, and $10$ correspond to $c=1$, $4$, and $36$, so that the reference's weight on the description, $c/(c+n)$, ranges from $0.2$ to $0.97$ over $n\leq4$. The noise level $\sigma=60$ makes this range possible with three-digit numbers: the smallest stated standard deviation, $10$, is still large relative to rounding, and the largest, $60$, is small relative to the range $100$ to $999$.

\paragraph{Conditions.}
We vary the stated precision. A more precise description also has a more accurate center, so we add a condition that changes only the statement: each $c=36$ task keeps its center and its demonstrations, and the prompt states $c=4$. Section~\ref{sec:llm-stated} adds a stated chance that the description is irrelevant, and Appendix~\ref{app:llm} varies the description's wording.

\paragraph{Models.}
We select models that can answer with reasoning enabled and with it disabled under the same model identifier. They include open-weight and proprietary models from several developers. The main comparison uses the same $200$ tasks for five such models: gpt-oss-20b \citep{openai2025}, Qwen3.5 9B \citep{qwen35}, Gemma~4 31B \citep{gemma4}, GPT-6 Sol \citep{openai2026sol}, and Claude Opus~4.6 \citep{anthropic2026opus}. We also evaluate another recent frontier model, Claude Haiku~5.5 \citep{anthropic2026haiku}, at medium effort; its reasoning could not be disabled in our setup, so it appears only with reasoning. GPT-6 Sol and the two Claude models are served through their developers' command-line agents, with tools disabled. Appendix~\ref{app:llm} gives execution and readout details and a larger run without reasoning on $2{,}000$ tasks.

\paragraph{Bayesian reference.}
Our \emph{prompt-based Bayesian reference} is the Bayesian predictor for the statistical model that the prompt states: its observation noise, its description precision, and, when given, its chance of irrelevance. Without a description, the prompt says only that the mean is “unknown”: a stated distribution for it would itself be a description, and the baseline should reflect only what a model already knows (Section~\ref{sec:setting}). The reference therefore needs a baseline prior of its own, and takes a uniform one over the integers $100$ to $999$, the range of every number shown in a prompt. With a description, the reference's prediction is approximately the precision-weighted Gaussian posterior mean \citep{neuenschwander2020},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-rule}
\hat w_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{c\,m+n\bar y}{c+n},
\end{equation}
where $\bar y$ is the demonstration mean. With a fixed input there is no dimension term: in the idealized continuous Gaussian model with a flat baseline prior, a correctly specified description has exact ESS $c$, its conventional prior ESS. The reference follows the stated values even where they differ from the generating values, so agreement with the reference measures how a model uses the stated information.

\paragraph{Measures.}
We compute ESS against each model's own baseline curve and report \emph{summary ESS}, its median over $n\in\{1,2,4\}$: models rarely answer with a number at $n=0$, and at large $n$, ESS mainly reflects how slowly their baseline curves fall (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). For the reference, summary ESS is $34.6$ at $c=36$, not exactly $36$, because numbers are rounded, tasks are redrawn, and ESS is interpolated (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). We also regress predictions on $m$, $\bar y$, and an intercept. The coefficient on $m$ is the \emph{fitted description weight}; the reference weight is $c/(c+n)$.

\subsection{Reasoning and Stated Precision}\label{sec:once}\label{sec:reasoning}

\paragraph{Precise descriptions are underweighted without reasoning.}
For $c=36$, summary ESS ranges from $2.0$ to $10.3$ across the five models without reasoning, compared with $34.6$ for the reference (Figure~\ref{fig:llm}b). The shortfall grows with precision: at $c=4$, the models reach $0.5$ to $2.7$, against $3.5$ for the reference (Figure~\ref{fig:llm}a), and at $c=1$ they reach $-0.2$ to $0.9$, against $1.0$ (Table~\ref{tab:reason}). The models also differ in how much they respond to the stated precision itself. When the prompt states $c=4$ for the $c=36$ tasks, reference ESS falls from $34.6$ to $13.7$. It stays above the $3.5$ of the $c=4$ tasks because the center is as accurate as before. Under the same change, GPT-6 Sol's ESS falls from $10.3$ to $2.4$, and the ESS of Opus~4.6 and of Gemma~4 31B falls by about half; Qwen3.5 9B and gpt-oss-20b change less (Table~\ref{tab:reason}).

\paragraph{Reasoning improves the use of stated precision.}
With reasoning enabled, Opus~4.6, GPT-6 Sol, and Gemma~4 31B attain approximately the reference ESS at both precisions (Figure~\ref{fig:llm}a,b), and their ESS falls with the reference's when only the stated precision is lowered (Table~\ref{tab:reason}). Haiku~5.5 matches the reference at $c=4$ and falls short at $c=36$, with summary ESS $28$ against $34.6$. The shortfall is largest at $n=1$, where its ESS is only $3.4$ and more than a quarter of its replies contain no recorded reasoning output. At $n=2$ its ESS matches the reference, and at $n=4$ it is $28$ against $33$. The individual replies of these four models follow the reference. A reply implies a description weight, $(\hat w-\bar y)/(m-\bar y)$. This ratio is well determined on tasks with $|m-\bar y|\geq30$; on those tasks at $n=4$, between $89\%$ and $100\%$ of each model's replies have a weight within $0.05$ of the reference's (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

Qwen3.5 9B and gpt-oss-20b also approach the reference weights. Their own baseline curves flatten at larger $n$ and never fall to the error these models reach with the description, so the squares in Figure~\ref{fig:llm}b measure ESS on the reference's baseline curve instead. gpt-oss-20b's ESS stays below the reference because about $2\%$ of its replies contain arithmetic errors (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). Reasoning does not suffice for every model. Olmo~3 7B Think \citep{olmo2025three} is a small open-weight model that always reasons, so it has no run without reasoning; its summary ESS at $c=36$ is $6.2$ (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

\subsection{Stated Reliability}\label{sec:llm-stated}

Section~\ref{sec:reliability} shows that uncertain relevance sharply lowers a description's value, and that demonstrations then carry evidence about relevance. To test both, we make the $c=36$ description unreliable and say so. The prompt adds that there is a $10\%$ chance that the statement is wrong, in which case the mean “was instead drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 100 to 999.” The stated chance is also the true one. Each task is run twice: once with its relevant description, and once with an irrelevant one, whose center is drawn independently of the task. We average the two errors with weights $0.9$ and $0.1$. Because of cost, this design covers two models, GPT-6 Sol and Haiku~5.5; Appendix~\ref{app:llm-reliability} also reports a $50\%$ chance.

For the reference, a one-in-ten chance of irrelevance reduces ESS from $34.6$ to $5.0$ (Figure~\ref{fig:llm}c), just as it reduces the Bayes-optimal ESS from $117$ to $13.5$ in Section~\ref{sec:reliability}. Most of what remains depends on checking the description against the demonstrations. Consider \emph{fixed-weight averaging}, a predictor that keeps the chance of irrelevance at the stated $10\%$, whatever the demonstrations show. Its ESS is only $0.3$, because it cannot discount a description that the demonstrations contradict. With reasoning, GPT-6 Sol reaches $4.8$, and its replies are within $2$ of the reference's prediction in $89\%$ of cases with a relevant description and $92\%$ with an irrelevant one. Haiku~5.5 reaches $3.2$; $85\%$ of its replies are within $2$ of the reference with a relevant description and $60\%$ with an irrelevant one. Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol's ESS is $0.9$, and at most $19\%$ of its replies are within $2$ of the reference.

A model can revise the stated chance in light of the demonstrations only if the prompt says how the mean is distributed when the description is irrelevant. An earlier version of the prompt said only that the mean was then “unknown”; with reasoning, most models applied the stated chance as given, without revising it (Appendix~\ref{app:llm-reliability}). The networks take a description's reliability from their training data. With reasoning, GPT-6 Sol takes it from the prompt and, as the reference does, revises it in light of the demonstrations; Haiku~5.5 does so less consistently.

\paragraph{A semantic task.}
Descriptions in practice usually state constraints in ordinary language, not distributions. We therefore also test the same two models on a message-filing task with such descriptions. The unreliable descriptions in this task are in fact irrelevant about half the time, and both models again agree with a Bayesian reference (Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}).

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Bayesian accounts of ICL.}
Theoretical work studies ICL as Bayesian inference over latent tasks \citep{xie2022,panwar2024}, as a learning algorithm implemented in a forward pass \citep{akyurek2023,vonoswald2023}, and as meta-learning across tasks \citep{garg2022,muller2022}. Meta-trained predictors can approximate Bayesian inference under suitable training conditions \citep{genewein2025}. Agreement with Bayesian predictors can depend on training-task diversity and on the function class \citep{raventos2023,panwar2024}, and language-model ICL can violate Bayesian consistency \citep{falck2024}. Beyond the forms of task information cited in Section~1, \citet{lin2024} study a prior that mixes task groups, and \citet{tong2026} study an instruction whose influence changes.

\paragraph{Prior effective sample size.}
Prior ESS expresses the influence of a prior in observation units, with different definitions matching different statistical quantities \citep{morita2008,neuenschwander2020}. Our comparison is closest to measures based on expected loss \citep{reimherr2021,wiesenfarth2020}. \citet{lescao2021} measure the average horizontal separation of learning curves in prompt-based fine-tuning. \citet{selby2025} compare priors elicited from language models with data-based predictors; their measure is the number of observations needed to match predictive loss. We apply loss matching to task descriptions, at each demonstration count and against each learner's own baseline curve.

\paragraph{Descriptions and evidence in language models.}
Task summaries distilled from demonstrations can replace the examples in context \citep{honda2025}. Other studies report weak sensitivity to instruction relevance \citep{webson2022} or limited benefits from repository instruction files \citep{gloaguen2026}. \citet{gupta2025} compare explicitly stated coin biases with observations and evaluate the predictions against Bayesian predictors. Concurrent work studies how reasoning and code execution affect language models' approximation of Bayesian posteriors \citep{filbry2026}; our main runs disable code execution. Appendix~\ref{app:related} discusses further connections.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

We asked how descriptions can be characterized and how well learners use them in practice. For the first question, we treated a description as a prior with a specificity and a reliability, and we measured it by its predictive ESS. A reliable description can replace many demonstrations; uncertain relevance imposes a finite ESS ceiling before demonstrations are observed; and demonstrations can then raise a description's ESS by supplying evidence about its relevance. For the second, meta-trained Transformers approach the Bayes-optimal ESS, and their fitted description weight follows the reliability of their training data. Most of the pretrained language models that we test need reasoning enabled to use a stated precision as the Bayesian reference does; when descriptions are unreliable and the prompt says so, GPT-6 Sol with reasoning also matches the reference.

The analysis explains, in a controlled setting, why descriptions sometimes substitute for many examples \citep{lescao2021,honda2025} and sometimes have limited influence \citep{webson2022,gupta2025,gloaguen2026}. A description can have little influence for three different reasons: it says little about the task, it may be wrong, or the learner does not use what it says. The Bayes-optimal ESS reflects the first two, and comparing a learner's ESS with it reveals the third. More broadly, because a description enters as a prior, the Bayesian analysis of ICL extends from demonstrations to descriptions.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
We develop the theory in Gaussian linear regression with squared error, a standard controlled setting for ICL \citep{garg2022}. Within it, we model a relevant description as a single Gaussian centered on one point. This is the usual case: a description points to one task and is more or less specific about it. Some descriptions take other forms: a description may be consistent with several distinct tasks, or its prior may have a different shape altogether. Future work could model such descriptions with a mixture of Gaussians, which can approximate a wide class of priors \citep{dalal1983}.

Our language-model prompts state a description's precision and reliability explicitly, as numbers, because only then can every prediction be compared with an exact reference. In practice, these properties are implicit. A more specific instruction is typically a longer one that says more about the task, as a step-by-step workflow does, and nothing in it states a precision. A model must infer such properties from the instruction itself. Our experiments take first steps in this direction: with informal wording such as “give or take,” models weight the description differently from the reference, and in the message-filing task, where a note's specificity lies in what it says, two models with reasoning agree with it (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). How models infer specificity and reliability from natural language is a natural next question, and one that predictive ESS can measure.

Our language-model experiments use small synthetic tasks, and the test of unreliable descriptions covers two models. Extending them to more models and to tasks closer to practice, as the message-filing task begins to do, would show how far the agreement with the Bayesian reference reaches. This agreement concerns a model's predictions; how models compute them is a question for future work.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how learners combine task descriptions with demonstrations. Its measurement framework may inform prompt evaluation and design when descriptions are uncertain or incomplete. The experiments use synthetic tasks; their results do not establish dependable instruction following in consequential applications.

\subsection{Reliable Descriptions}\label{app:specificity}

\paragraph{Notation and risk.}
Write $\varepsilon = 1/a_0$ for the baseline prior precision. Let $X_n\in\R^{n\times d}$ contain the demonstration inputs as rows, and set $W_n=X_n^\top X_n$. For a prior precision $\kappa>0$, define
\[
R^{\kappa}(n)=\E\operatorname{tr}(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
Thus $R^{\mathrm{demo}}=R^{\varepsilon}$ and $R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}=R^c$. Here and throughout, risk means expected squared prediction error against the noise-free query response $w^\top x$. The identities follow from the Gaussian posterior covariance: the posterior mean minimizes conditional squared error, and the independent query satisfies $\E[xx^\top]=I_d$. Averaging the conditional error $x^\top\Sigma x$ therefore gives $\E\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.

Throughout the proofs, $d$ is a positive integer, $a_0>0$, $c\ge\varepsilon$, $r=1/(ca_0)\in(0,1]$, and $p\in[0,1]$. Specificity is represented by the relevant component's precision $c$; $r$ is its variance as a fraction of the baseline variance. Reliability $p$ is the prior probability of relevance. Noninteger demonstration counts refer to linear interpolation between the risks at adjacent integers.

\paragraph{Wishart identities.}
We use three standard properties of $W_n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(W\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
    \item If $n<d$, then $W_n$ has rank $n$ almost surely. Consequently, $(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}$ has $d-n$ eigenvalues equal to $1/\kappa$.
    \item If $n\ge d$ and $e$ is a fixed unit vector, then $(e^\top W_n^{-1}e)^{-1}\sim\chi^2_{n-d+1}$ \citep[Theorem~3.2.12]{muirhead1982}. Hence
    \[
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-1}=\frac{d}{n-d-1}\quad(n\ge d+2),
    \]
    whereas this expectation is infinite for $n=d$ and $n=d+1$.
    \item For fixed $d$, as $n\to\infty$,
    \[
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-2}=\frac{d}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
    \qquad
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-3}=O(n^{-3})
    \]
    \citep{vonrosen1988}.
\end{enumerate}
The Sherman--Morrison identity implies that an additional demonstration strictly decreases the expected trace of the posterior covariance. Thus $R^\kappa(n)$ is strictly decreasing, and (W2) implies that it tends to zero. Its linear interpolation is therefore continuous and strictly decreasing. In the correctly specified joint model, conditioning on the description cannot increase expected posterior variance, so $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)\le R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)$ and the Bayes-optimal ESS is nonnegative.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reliable}]
A reliable description without demonstrations has risk
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(0)=\frac{d}{c}=ra_0d.
\]

\emph{(i) Fixed variance reduction.}
By (W1), $R^\varepsilon(n)\ge(d-n)a_0$ for $n<d$; the inequality also holds trivially for $n\ge d$. Linear interpolation preserves this bound, so
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)\ge(d-t)a_0\qquad(t\ge0).
\]
For $t<(1-r)d$, the right-hand side exceeds $ra_0d$. The matching sample size must therefore satisfy $\ess_0\ge(1-r)d$.

For every integer $n$,
\[
\frac{R^\varepsilon(n)}{a_0}
=\E\operatorname{tr}(I_d+a_0W_n)^{-1}
\longrightarrow(d-n)^+
\qquad\text{as }a_0\to\infty.
\]
Indeed, an eigenvalue $1/(1+a_0\lambda)$ is bounded by one and converges to the indicator of $\lambda=0$, so dominated convergence and the almost-sure rank of $W_n$ apply. The same limit holds at every real $t$ by interpolation. For fixed $r\in(0,1)$, the limiting curve crosses $rd$ at the unique point $t=(1-r)d$. Strict monotonicity then gives convergence of the crossing. For $r=1$, the description is uninformative and $\ess_0=0$ for every $a_0$, giving the same conclusion.

\emph{(ii) Fixed precision.}
For fixed $c>0$, the description risk $d/c$ is independent of $a_0$. As $a_0\to\infty$, monotone convergence, (W1), and (W2) give
\[
R^\varepsilon(n)\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
+\infty,&n\le d+1,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d}{n-d-1},&n\ge d+2.
\end{cases}
\]
If $c$ is a positive integer, the finite part of this limiting curve equals $d/c$ at the integer $n=c+d+1$. For every fixed point below this crossing the limiting risk is larger than $d/c$, and for every fixed point above it the limiting risk is smaller. The crossing of the interpolated finite-$a_0$ risk therefore converges to $c+d+1$. The integer condition ensures that the equality occurs at a grid point; linear interpolation does not in general preserve the same expression for noninteger $c$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Large-sample expansion.}
For fixed $d$ and $\kappa$, commuting $W_n$ with $\kappa I_d$ gives
\[
(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}
=W_n^{-1}-\kappa W_n^{-2}
+\kappa^2W_n^{-2}(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
The final term is positive semidefinite and bounded above by $\kappa^2W_n^{-3}$. Using (W2) and (W3),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extra}
R^\kappa(n)=\frac{d}{n-d-1}-\frac{\kappa d}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
An additional demonstration reduces the leading term by $d/n^2+O(n^{-3})$. A fixed increase in prior precision and a fixed increase in the demonstration count therefore reduce risk at the same asymptotic order. This comparison gives the large-sample ESS limits below.

\subsection{Unreliable Descriptions}\label{app:reliability}

Let $\mathcal P_n=(D_n,m,x)$ denote the full prompt, including the query input. The posterior relevance probability is $\pi_n=P(Z=1\mid\mathcal P_n)$, whereas $p$ remains the prior relevance probability. Conditional on $Z=1$, the posterior parameters are
\[
\Sigma_1=(cI_d+W_n)^{-1},
\qquad
\mu_1=\Sigma_1(c m+X_n^\top y_{1:n}).
\]
Conditional on $Z=0$, the description center $m$ is independent of $w$, and
\[
\Sigma_0=(\varepsilon I_d+W_n)^{-1},
\qquad
\mu_0=\Sigma_0X_n^\top y_{1:n}.
\]
The posterior relevance probability follows from the two Gaussian marginal likelihoods,
\begin{align*}
L_1&=\mathcal N\!\left(y_{1:n};X_nm,I_n+c^{-1}X_nX_n^\top\right),\\
L_0&=\mathcal N\!\left(y_{1:n};0,I_n+a_0X_nX_n^\top\right),\\
\pi_n&=\frac{pL_1}{pL_1+(1-p)L_0}.
\end{align*}
The query input does not enter this update because it is independent of $(Z,m,D_n)$. Without demonstrations, both marginal likelihoods equal one and $\pi_0=p$, since $m$ has the same distribution under both relevance states. At $p=0$ or $p=1$, the posterior contains only the corresponding component. At $r=1$, $m=0$ almost surely and the two components coincide, so the description provides no additional task information for any $p$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:unreliable}]
As in Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999}, the posterior mean prediction averages the component predictions using their posterior probabilities:
\[
\E[w^\top x\mid\mathcal P_n]
=\big(\pi_n\mu_1+(1-\pi_n)\mu_0\big)^\top x.
\]
The law of total variance over $Z$ separates posterior variance into within-component and between-component terms:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Var}(w^\top x\mid\mathcal P_n)
={}&\pi_n x^\top\Sigma_1x+(1-\pi_n)x^\top\Sigma_0x\\
&+\pi_n(1-\pi_n)\big((\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top x\big)^2.
\end{align*}
For $0<p<1$ and any integrable function $g$ of the prompt,
\[
\E[\pi_ng(\mathcal P_n)]=\E[Zg(\mathcal P_n)]
=p\,\E[g(\mathcal P_n)\mid Z=1].
\]
Conditional on $Z=1$, the prompt follows the reliable-description model. Conditional on $Z=0$, the description provides no information about the task. Taking expectations in the variance decomposition therefore gives
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)
=pR^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)+(1-p)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)+\mathcal V_n,
\]
with $\mathcal V_n$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp}. The endpoint cases $p=0$ and $p=1$ follow directly from the single-component posteriors.

At $n=0$, $\mu_1=m$, $\mu_0=0$, and the prediction is $p\,m^\top x$. For each coordinate,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(w_i)=a_0,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(m_i)=(1-r)a_0,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(w_i,m_i)=p(1-r)a_0.
\]
It follows that
\[
\E(w_i-pm_i)^2
=a_0-2p^2(1-r)a_0+p^2(1-r)a_0
=\big(1-p^2(1-r)\big)a_0.
\]
Since $\E[xx^\top]=I_d$, the prediction risk is $\E\|w-pm\|^2$. Summing over coordinates proves Equation~\eqref{eq:zero}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}]
\emph{(i) No demonstrations.}
For $0<p<1$ and finite $c$,
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(0)
=\big(1-p^2(1-r)\big)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0)
>(1-p^2)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0).
\]
The risk on the left decreases to the right-hand side as $c\to\infty$, equivalently $r\to0$. The interpolated baseline risk is continuous, strictly decreasing, and tends to zero. Consequently, the matching sample size is strictly below the unique finite $n_p^{\sharp}$ defined in the corollary and converges to it as $c\to\infty$. At $p=0$, the description is always irrelevant, so $\ess_0=n_0^{\sharp}=0$; the non-strict bound remains valid.

The baseline lower bound and normalized limit established in Appendix~\ref{app:specificity} also give
\[
\ess_0\ge p^2(1-r)d,
\qquad
\ess_0\longrightarrow p^2(1-r)d
\]
when $a_0\to\infty$ with $r$ fixed. Applying the same argument to the level $1-p^2$ gives $n_p^{\sharp}\to p^2d$. These expressions are limits as the baseline prior becomes diffuse; they need not hold exactly at finite $a_0$.

For comparison, an oracle predictor that observes $Z$ has zero-demonstration risk
\[
\big(1-p(1-r)\big)a_0d\ge(1-p)a_0d.
\]
The oracle's zero-demonstration ESS ceiling therefore approaches $pd$ as $a_0\to\infty$. For the learner that must infer relevance, this limit is $p^2d$. The difference is the cost of uncertainty about relevance.

\emph{(ii) Many demonstrations.}
Fix $d$, $a_0$, and $c$, and define the oracle risk
\[
L(n)=pR^c(n)+(1-p)R^\varepsilon(n).
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:extra} implies, uniformly over integers $0\le t\le T=\lceil c\rceil+1$,
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n+t)-L(n)
=\frac{d}{n^2}\left[p(c-\varepsilon)-t+O(n^{-1})\right].
\]
Linear interpolation gives the same expansion for real $t\in[0,T]$. Let $n+\eta_n$ be the sample size at which the baseline risk equals $L(n)$. Then
\[
\eta_n=p(c-\varepsilon)+O(n^{-1}).
\]
When $p(c-\varepsilon)=0$, the two risks coincide and $\eta_n=0$ exactly. If $p=1$, the description risk equals $L(n)$, so
\[
\ess_n\longrightarrow c-\varepsilon=(1-r)c.
\]
If $p<1$, the variance decomposition gives $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)=L(n)+\mathcal V_n\ge L(n)$. Since the baseline risk decreases with sample size, $\ess_n\le\eta_n$, proving
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ess_n\le p(c-\varepsilon)=p(1-r)c.
\]
Because $\mathcal V_n$ is nonnegative and need not vanish at the required rate, the result for unreliable descriptions is an upper bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Partial Descriptions}\label{app:partial}

Descriptions often specify only part of a task. In our model, a reliable description may constrain only $k<d$ directions. With fixed $c>0$, its zero-demonstration ESS approaches $k$ as $a_0\to\infty$: the unconstrained directions dominate the remaining error. With many demonstrations, its ESS instead approaches $k(c-a_0^{-1})/d$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}). A sufficiently precise partial description can therefore gain value as demonstrations resolve what it leaves unspecified. At $d=16$, $k=8$, and $c=100$, ESS rises from $8.1$ without demonstrations to $36.2$ after $100$ demonstrations (Table~\ref{tab:partial}).

Formally, suppose the description leaves a fraction $r$ of the baseline variance in these directions and leaves the remaining $d-k$ directions unchanged. The description-conditioned prior precision is $c$ in the constrained directions and $\varepsilon=1/a_0$ elsewhere. Write
\[
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(cI_k,\varepsilon I_{d-k}).
\]
The prediction risk is $\E\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}$. Rotational invariance of $W_n$ means that the result depends on the number of constrained directions, not on their orientation.

\begin{proposition}[ESS of a partial description]\label{prop:partial}
Let $p=1$ and let $0\le k<d$ be an integer.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item[(i)] As $a_0\to\infty$, $\ess_0\to(1-r)k$ with $r$ fixed, and $\ess_0\to k$ with $c$ fixed.
    \item[(ii)] For fixed $d$, $a_0$, and $c$, as $n\to\infty$,
    \[
    \ess_n\longrightarrow\frac{k}{d}(c-1/a_0)
    =\frac{k}{d}(1-r)c.
    \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

As the baseline prior becomes diffuse with $c$ fixed, the unconstrained directions dominate the zero-demonstration risk, and ESS approaches the number of constrained directions, $k$. After many demonstrations, ESS instead approaches the added precision averaged across all $d$ directions. A sufficiently precise partial description can therefore be worth more alongside demonstrations than by itself. Table~\ref{tab:partial} illustrates this complementarity: at $c=100$, a description of half of the $d=16$ directions has ESS $8.1$ without demonstrations and $36.2$ with $100$ demonstrations.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}]
\emph{(i) No demonstrations.}
The description risk is
\[
\operatorname{tr}\Lambda^{-1}=(kr+d-k)a_0.
\]
Using $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)\ge(d-t)a_0$ gives $\ess_0\ge(1-r)k$. The normalized baseline risk converges to $(d-t)^+$, locally uniformly in $t$, by the integer limits and linear interpolation. With $r$ fixed, the normalized description risk is $d-(1-r)k$. With $c$ fixed, it is
\[
d-k+\frac{k}{ca_0}\longrightarrow d-k.
\]
Because $k<d$, both limiting levels are positive and have unique crossings, at $(1-r)k$ and $k$, respectively. Convergence of the crossings proves the claim. When the description leaves the prior unchanged, its ESS is identically zero.

\emph{(ii) Many demonstrations.}
Applying the resolvent identity twice gives
\[
(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}
=W_n^{-1}-W_n^{-1}\Lambda W_n^{-1}
+W_n^{-1}\Lambda W_n^{-1}\Lambda(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
Rotational invariance implies
\[
\E W_n^{-2}=\frac{\E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-2}}{d}I_d.
\]
The expected trace of the middle term is therefore
$\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)/n^2+O(n^{-3})$ by (W3). To bound the remainder, use $|\operatorname{tr}(MB)|\le\|M\|_{\mathrm{op}}\operatorname{tr}B$ for $B\succeq0$, together with $(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}\preceq W_n^{-1}$. Its absolute trace is at most
\[
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\|W_n^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-1}
\le d\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-3},
\]
whose expectation is $O(n^{-3})$. Consequently,
\[
\E\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}
=\frac{d}{n-d-1}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Lambda}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
Matching this expansion to the baseline risk, as in Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}(ii), gives
\[
\ess_n\longrightarrow\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Lambda-d\varepsilon}{d}
=\frac{k(c-\varepsilon)}{d}.
\]
\end{proof}
The same expansion applies to any fixed Gaussian description precision matrix $\Lambda\succeq\varepsilon I_d$ compatible with the baseline task distribution. Its large-sample ESS is the added precision averaged across directions, $(\operatorname{tr}\Lambda-d\varepsilon)/d$.

\subsection{Assuming Relevance Under Misspecification}\label{app:misspecification}

A learner that has only seen relevant descriptions, such as a network trained at $p=1$ (Section~\ref{sec:networks}), has no reason to discount one. We ask what assuming relevance costs when descriptions are in fact unreliable. Consider the Bayesian predictor that sets $p=1$ in its inference model and therefore returns $\mu_1^\top x$. We evaluate its risk, denoted $R^{\mathrm{assume}}(n)$, under the data-generating model with relevance probability $p$. The assumed prior is misspecified when $p<1$ and $r<1$, because it omits the irrelevant component.

Without demonstrations, this predictor returns $m^\top x$. The moments used in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:unreliable} give
\[
R^{\mathrm{assume}}(0)
=\E\|w-m\|^2
=\big(1-(2p-1)(1-r)\big)a_0d.
\]
For $r<1$, this risk exceeds the demonstrations-only risk $a_0d$ exactly when $p<1/2$. At $r=1$, the description carries no information, $m=0$ almost surely, and the two risks coincide for every $p$.

With demonstrations, the predictor continues to use $\mu_1^\top x$ regardless of $Z$, where
\[
\mu_1=m+(cI_d+W_n)^{-1}X_n^\top(y_{1:n}-X_nm).
\]
For fixed $a_0$ and $n$, $\mu_1\to m$ in mean square as $c\to\infty$. Conditional on relevance, $\E\|w-m\|^2=ra_0d\to0$; conditional on irrelevance, it is $(2-r)a_0d\to2a_0d$. Thus
\[
\lim_{c\to\infty}R^{\mathrm{assume}}(n)=2(1-p)a_0d.
\]
For every $p<1$, this is a positive risk floor at fixed $n$, whereas $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)\to0$ as $n\to\infty$. Hence a sufficiently precise description can increase risk relative to the demonstrations-only predictor when relevance is assumed despite $p<1$. At $p=0.9$, $c=100$, $d=16$, and $a_0=10$, the predictor assuming relevance has risk $25.9$, compared with $26.6$ without the description, at $n=14$. At $n=15$, these risks are $25.5$ and $19.5$, respectively. Its risk remains higher at every larger demonstration count evaluated, up to $10^5$. The order of limits matters: the preceding argument takes $c\to\infty$ at fixed $n$; it does not imply that demonstrations cannot overcome a fixed finite prior precision.

\paragraph{Architecture and optimization.}
The Transformer has $12$ layers, $8$ attention heads, and width $256$, matching the model size of \citet{garg2022}. We train with Adam at learning rate $10^{-4}$ for $40{,}000$ steps, using batches of $1{,}024$ newly sampled prompts and no curriculum. Following \citet{zhu2026}, the model predicts a mean and a log variance for the query label and minimizes log loss. The optimal predicted mean is the posterior mean, which also minimizes squared error. We evaluate the predicted mean using the loss in Equation~\eqref{eq:regret}.

Each demonstration token contains an input and its label. Two indicator coordinates distinguish token types, and the model uses no positional encoding. When a description is absent, its token is masked out of attention. The prediction is read from the query token. Labels and description centers are divided by $\sqrt{a_0}$, gradients are clipped to norm $1$, and training uses bf16 autocast. The demonstration count is sampled uniformly from $0$ to $20$, except for the $c = 0.2$ and $c = 2$ experiments, which use $0$ to $15$, and the $d = 10$ experiments, which use $0$ to $40$.

Layers use pre-normalization, GELU activations, feed-forward width $1{,}024$, and no dropout. At $d = 5$, the model has $9.5$ million parameters. Adam uses the PyTorch defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, with no weight decay and a constant learning rate. Training seeds are $0$, $1$, and $2$ for $c = 10$, for $d = 10$, and for the partial-description experiments; $0$ and $1$ for $c = 1$; and $0$ for $c \in \{0.2,2\}$. A $d = 5$ network takes approximately $80$ minutes to train on one NVIDIA L4 GPU, and a $d = 10$ network takes approximately $2.8$ hours. Training all $25$ networks takes approximately $38$ GPU-hours.

Without a description, every $d = 5$ network's error is within $0.21$ of the Bayes-optimal error of approximately $50$ at $n = 0$, and within $0.32$ on average over its training demonstration counts. At $d = 10$, the corresponding gaps are at most $0.50$ relative to an error of approximately $100$, and $0.41$. With a description at $c = 10$, $p = 1$, and $n = 0$, network error ranges from $0.67$ to $0.71$, compared with $0.49$ for the Bayes-optimal predictor. The networks therefore remove $98.6\%$ of the baseline error, compared with $99.0\%$ for the Bayes-optimal predictor.

\paragraph{Additional specificity levels.}
Four additional networks cover $c = 0.2$ and $c = 2$, each at $p = 1$ and $p = 0.9$. Their zero-demonstration ESS values are within $0.03$ of the Bayes-optimal values evaluated on the same prompts: $2.64$ versus $2.66$, $2.16$ versus $2.15$, $7.32$ versus $7.35$, and $4.38$ versus $4.37$, respectively.

\paragraph{Higher dimension and partial descriptions.}
At $d = 10$, we train three seeds with description precision $c = 10$ and extend training and evaluation to $40$ demonstrations so that the baseline curve reaches the ESS crossing. Network ESS is $17.8$, compared with a Bayes-optimal value of $20.8$ and the limiting expression $c + d + 1 = 21$. As at $d = 5$, the networks capture most of the description's predictive value but fall short of the Bayes-optimal ESS.

We also train networks with a partial description, as defined in Appendix~\ref{app:partial}. The description token contains $m$ in the first $k = 3$ of the $d = 5$ coordinates and zeros elsewhere; the remaining task coordinates follow the baseline prior. For this $c = 10$ description, mean ESS across three seeds increases from $3.27$ at $n = 0$ to $5.55$ at $n = 10$. The corresponding Bayes-optimal values are $3.22$ and $5.64$. This increase agrees with the behavior described by Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}.

\paragraph{ESS with demonstrations.}
Figure~\ref{fig:networks-ess-n} evaluates each network at its training reliability. For $c = 1$, network ESS remains within $0.6$ of the Bayes-optimal value at every demonstration count whose crossing lies within the evaluation grid. For $c = 10$, the largest shortfalls are $2.4$, $2.9$, and $1.4$ demonstrations at $p = 1$, $0.99$, and $0.9$, respectively; the largest excess is $0.3$. At $n = 5$, the three $c = 10$ networks trained at $p = 0.9$ have ESS values of $4.83$, $4.79$, and $4.77$, compared with a Bayes-optimal value of $5.20$ on the same prompts.

\paragraph{Reliability shift after demonstrations are observed.}
For $c = 10$, networks trained at $p = 1$ and $p = 0.99$ both have fitted description weights near one at $n = 0$. With demonstrations, their responses to a shift in test reliability differ. At test reliability $p = 0.9$, the description reduces error for networks trained at $p = 0.99$ at every evaluated $n$, as it does for the Bayes-optimal predictor under the test distribution. For networks trained at $p = 1$, the description instead increases error from six demonstrations onward in every seed. The excess error ranges from $0.77$ to $1.06$ at $n = 6$ and peaks between $2.74$ and $2.94$ at $n = 9$. A Bayesian predictor that assumes relevance ($p = 1$) also first has higher error than the demonstrations-only predictor at $n = 6$, with excess error $1.12$ there and $2.96$ at $n = 9$ (Appendix~\ref{app:misspecification}), so these networks behave as if every description were relevant.

\section{Additional Language-Model Results and Evaluation Details}\label{app:llm}

To estimate behavior without reasoning more precisely and across more models, we generate $2{,}000$ tasks. Six open-weight models answer all tasks without reasoning; we refer to these experiments as the \emph{full run}. The models are gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.5 9B, and Gemma~4 31B, which also appear in Section~\ref{sec:llm}, together with Phi-4 (14B; \citealp{abdin2024}), OLMo~2 32B Instruct \citep{walsh2025}, and Gemma~4 12B. The main numerical experiments in Section~\ref{sec:llm} use the first $200$ of these tasks. The semantic experiment uses a separate set of $200$ tasks, described in Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}. Unless stated otherwise, the results in this appendix use the full run without reasoning.

Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports summary ESS, defined as the median over $n \in \{1,2,4\}$, with uncertainty intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the underlying learning curves. Tables~\ref{tab:llm-worth} and~\ref{tab:llm-worth-written} report ESS at every evaluated demonstration count; Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} compares interpolation methods; and Table~\ref{tab:llm-full} reports description weights for the models with a distribution readout.

For the numerical experiments, the \emph{prompt-based Bayesian reference} combines the probabilistic statements in the prompt with an assumed baseline prior when the prompt does not specify one. It is optimal under that assumed model, but need not minimize error under the actual task distribution when the two differ. We therefore distinguish this reference from the correctly specified Bayesian predictor in the theory. The semantic experiment specifies a finite task distribution and uses its exact Bayesian predictor.

In the numerical task, $c=\sigma^2/\tau^2$ is the prior precision expressed in units of one demonstration. We distinguish the precision used to generate a description value from the precision stated in the prompt whenever they differ. Similarly, a stated reliability supplies a prior relevance probability to the model; it need not equal the proportion of relevant descriptions in the evaluation sample.

\subsection{Prompts, Tasks, and Model Readouts}

\paragraph{Model inclusion.}
We retain a model only if it produces a number in at least three quarters of replies overall and, averaged over $n=1$, $2$, and $4$ and both noise levels, in at least half of replies without a description. The latter responses determine the baseline curve used for summary ESS. This criterion excludes MiniCPM5~2B \citep{minicpm5}, OLMo~2 7B Instruct, and OLMo~2 13B Instruct, whose replies begin with a number in $62\%$, $20\%$, and $10\%$ of cases, respectively. These models often begin with explanations such as ``To predict\ldots'' or ``Given\ldots'' despite the numeric-only instruction.

The full run uses Hugging Face Transformers at each model's native precision, with checkpoints \path{openai/gpt-oss-20b}, \path{microsoft/phi-4}, \path{allenai/OLMo-2-0325-32B-Instruct}, \path{Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B}, \path{google/gemma-4-12B-it}, and \path{google/gemma-4-31B-it}. Tasks are sampled by a seeded generator. Reasoning is disabled where the chat template supports this option. For gpt-oss-20b, generation begins in the final channel, after the channel normally used for reasoning.

\paragraph{Distribution and written readouts.}
For gpt-oss-20b, Phi-4, and OLMo~2 32B without reasoning, the prediction is the mean of the first-token distribution, renormalized over the $900$ three-digit number tokens. We call this the \emph{distribution readout}. Other models, and all reasoning runs, use the number in the final answer, called the \emph{written readout}. We also evaluate written readouts for the three distribution-readout models as a robustness check (Table~\ref{tab:llm} for gpt-oss-20b, and Table~\ref{tab:paraphrase}). OLMo~2 32B assigns $41\%$ of first-token probability to numbers without a description at $n = 1$, and $75\%$ at $n = 2$. Its distribution readout at $n = 1$ therefore summarizes a minority of the first-token probability mass.

\paragraph{Prompt.}
The following text is supplied as the user turn in the model's chat template:
\begin{quote}\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
The numbers below are drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 60.\\
Its mean was drawn from a normal distribution with mean 510 and standard deviation 30.\\[3pt]
548\\
431\\[3pt]
Predict the next number. Reply with the number only.
\end{quote}

Without a description, the second sentence is ``Its mean is unknown.'' To state unreliability, a third sentence is added: ``There is a 10\% chance that this statement is wrong, in which case the mean is unknown.'' In the rerun that states the baseline prior, the final clause becomes ``in which case the mean was instead drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 100 to 999.'' With no demonstrations, the final question is ``Predict the first number.''

\paragraph{Task generation.}
A relevant description value is sampled conditional on the task so that the joint distribution satisfies $m \sim \mathcal{N}(500,100^2-\tau^2)$ and $w\mid m \sim \mathcal{N}(m,\tau^2)$. A task is redrawn if a displayed number or description value falls outside $100$ to $999$; this occurs for less than $1\%$ of tasks. The full run also includes $\sigma = 30$ conditions. Both standard deviations stated in the prompt are then halved, preserving $c \in \{1,4,36\}$. The qualitative pattern is unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:llm-worth}).

\paragraph{Numeric response rates.}
For the distribution readout, the numeric response rate is the probability mass assigned to number tokens at the first position. For the written readout, it is the fraction of replies containing a three-digit number. Tables~\ref{tab:paraphrase}, \ref{tab:llm-worth}, \ref{tab:llm-worth-written}, and~\ref{tab:llm-full} report this quantity as ``number''. Tables~\ref{tab:paraphrase} and~\ref{tab:llm-full} italicize cells whose rate is below three quarters.

\paragraph{Written-readout implementation and checks.}
The written readout extracts the number in the last \verb|\boxed{}| expression in the final answer, if present. Otherwise, it takes the last number with three digits before an optional decimal part, retaining the decimals. A digit group after a thousands separator, as in $14{,}945$, does not count as a qualifying number. In the full run, decoding is greedy with a limit of eight new tokens, so the reply is the number itself. Those predictions were recorded using an earlier readout that took the first qualifying number and retained only its integer part. Replies without a qualifying number are excluded from both error curves and weight regressions; the reported response rates identify the extent of this exclusion.

For gpt-oss-20b, written-readout weights differ from distribution-readout weights by at most $0.21$ across all conditions and demonstration counts. For $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$, written-readout summary ESS is $0.2$, $2.0$, and $3.7$, compared with $0.9$, $2.0$, and $2.8$ from the distribution readout. A single greedy response is a noisier summary than the distribution mean. Phi-4 produces a number in only $58\%$ of written replies across all conditions, so Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports only its distribution readout. In the wording experiment, however, its numeric response rate exceeds three quarters for the original wording. OLMo~2 32B produces a number in only $5\%$, $36\%$, and $48\%$ of written replies without a description at $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$, respectively, with $\sigma = 60$; we therefore do not report its written-readout ESS. Its written-readout weights for the three relevant descriptions at $n = 1$ are $0.83$, $0.81$, and $0.83$, compared with distribution-readout weights of $0.72$, $0.70$, and $0.70$.

\subsection{ESS Estimation and the Bayesian Reference}

\paragraph{Measurement.}
We apply Definition~\ref{def:ess} to squared error against the hidden mean. In the full run, the baseline curve is evaluated at $n = 1$, $2$, $4$, $\ldots$, $64$. We interpolate squared error, rather than its square root, linearly in $\log n$. The ESS at $n$ is the first interpolated demonstration count $t$ that matches the description-conditioned error, minus $n$. For a decreasing baseline curve, a negative ESS indicates higher error with the description than without it at the same demonstration count. For the Bayes-optimal predictor under the correctly specified model, conditioning on a description cannot increase expected risk, so its ESS is nonnegative.

If the description-conditioned error lies below the entire baseline curve up to $t_{\max}$, we report $\ess_n > t_{\max}-n$. Because the curve starts at one demonstration, we use the marker $\ess_n \leq 1-n$ when one demonstration alone already gives lower error. These markers record censoring at the evaluation boundaries; they do not define an extrapolated matching count or an exact ESS when the crossing does not exist. Summary ESS uses $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$. We exclude $n = 0$ because models rarely return a number without a description or demonstrations. At large $n$, ESS is strongly affected by slow baseline learning and excessive weight on the $c = 1$ and $c = 4$ descriptions. At $\sigma = 60$ and $n = 64$, model root-mean-square error without a description ranges from $9$ to $20$, compared with $7.5$ for the reference. A learner with a flatter baseline curve can assign a larger ESS to the same error reduction. Description-weight estimates complement ESS because they do not use that curve.

Bootstrap replicates resample tasks jointly for the description-conditioned and baseline curves and recompute the complete ESS statistic. The intervals therefore measure variation across tasks; they do not include interpolation bias, quantified below. We use $500$ replicates with a fixed seed and the same resampled tasks for every model.

\paragraph{Reference prior and exact sample equivalence.}
The prompt-based Bayesian reference takes ``unknown'' to mean uniform over $100$ to $999$. Without a description, its prediction is therefore approximately the demonstration mean, with negligible boundary truncation for the evaluated prompts. For a relevant Gaussian description with correctly stated precision, the idealized continuous model with a flat baseline prior gives exact ESS $c$. Integer rounding, finite-range truncation, and the task-rejection rule are not part of that exact identity; we compute the reported reference values on the same generated tasks as the models. When a chance of being wrong is stated, the reference uses it as the prior probability of irrelevance and computes the posterior relevance probability from the demonstrations. Otherwise, it treats the description as relevant.

Applying the empirical ESS procedure to the full-run reference gives $1.3$, $4.4$, and $36$ for $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$ at $\sigma = 60$, and $1.2$, $4.7$, and $43$ at $\sigma = 30$. Interpolation in $\log n$ induces an upward bias, while finite-task sampling can shift the estimate in either direction. We use the same estimator for the reference and the models. On the $200$ tasks of Section~\ref{sec:llm}, the grid additionally includes $n = 40$ and $48$ near the $c = 36$ crossing, and the reference values are $1.0$, $3.5$, and $34.6$. With a stated chance of irrelevance, the reference's summary ESS varies more across task samples: on $20{,}000$ tasks it is $7.3$ at a stated $50\%$ and $23.4$ at $10\%$, compared with $5.8$ and $22$ on the full run's $2{,}000$ tasks and $5.7$ and $27.5$ on the first $200$. Each model is compared with the reference on the same tasks.

The actual task prior, $\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$, is not stated in the prompt. A reference using that prior would have baseline precision equivalent to $0.36$ demonstrations at $\sigma = 60$ and $0.09$ at $\sigma = 30$. At $\sigma = 60$, the corresponding idealized continuous Gaussian model would give a relevant description exact ESS $c-0.36$, namely $0.64$, $3.64$, and $35.64$. This is the scalar analogue of $c-a_0^{-1}$ in Section~\ref{sec:specificity}. We use the prompt-based reference because the models are not given the actual task prior. This distinction matters under misspecification: unlike the correctly specified Bayesian predictor in Sections~\ref{sec:specificity}--\ref{sec:reliability}, a reference that incorrectly assumes an irrelevant description to be relevant can have negative ESS, indicating increased prediction error \citep{reimherr2021}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to interpolation.}
The full-run grid doubles $n$, so interpolation can affect ESS. On the reference's idealized errors, $\sigma^2/n$ without a description and $\sigma^2/(c+n)$ with one, interpolation in $\log n$ overestimates ESS by $0.2$, $0.3$, and $1.8$ for $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$. Linear interpolation in $n$ gives overestimates of $0.3$, $0.6$, and $4.1$.

For the models without reasoning, the relevant crossings are unique. Among the twelve baseline curves across the two noise levels, only Phi-4 at $\sigma = 60$ increases, slightly, from $20.3$ at $n = 32$ to $20.4$ at $n = 64$. Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} compares four estimators: interpolation in $\log n$; linear interpolation in $n$; a least-squares non-increasing fit using the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm; and a relative-least-squares fit of $A/(n+n_0)+B$ to the baseline errors. The final form is a Bayesian learning curve plus an error floor and recovers exact ESS when fit to the reference's idealized errors.

Across these choices, no model's summary ESS changes by more than $1.4$ demonstrations. For the $c = 36$ description, no model exceeds $8.1$, while the reference ranges from $35$ to $38$. Differences become larger at higher demonstration counts, where a flat learning curve turns a small error difference into a large change in ESS. For example, gpt-oss-20b at $n = 8$ and $c = 36$ has ESS $17$ under the estimator in Table~\ref{tab:llm-worth}, but $9.2$ under the fitted curve.

\subsection{Irrelevant Descriptions and Wording}

Apart from the matched design of Section~\ref{sec:llm-stated}, the experiments in the main text keep every description relevant, and all of them state its precision in one wording. This section relaxes each of these choices in the full run.

\paragraph{Irrelevant descriptions.}
An irrelevant description uses an independently drawn value. At $c = 36$, these descriptions have negative ESS for every model and for the prompt-based reference, which treats them as relevant. The models assign less weight to the description and consequently incur lower error in this condition: at $n = 4$, their root-mean-square errors range from $35$ to $64$, compared with $124$ for the reference.

\paragraph{Wording without reasoning.}
Descriptions in practice express uncertainty in many ways. To test whether the results depend on the original wording, we repeat the three relevant-description conditions and the no-description baseline at $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$ using three alternative description sentences: ``Its mean is about $m$, give or take $\tau$.'', ``Mean: $m \pm \tau$'', and ``An earlier estimate put its mean at $m$, with a standard error of $\tau$.'' Table~\ref{tab:paraphrase} uses the full-run tasks and written readouts.

Wording substantially changes the weight assigned to the description. For Gemma~4 12B with $c = 1$ and $n = 1$, this weight is $0.34$ under the original wording and $1.00$ under each alternative. For gpt-oss-20b, it ranges from $0.17$ to $0.67$. A fitted coefficient above $1$ means that, holding the demonstration mean fixed in the regression, a unit change in the description value corresponds to more than a unit change in the prediction. Nevertheless, none of the tested wordings yields the reference's sensitivity to prior precision: across models and wordings, the weight difference between $c = 36$ and $c = 1$ is at most $0.25$ at $n = 1$ and $0.44$ at $n = 4$, compared with reference differences of $0.47$ and $0.70$. The informal descriptions do not uniquely specify a probability distribution, so this comparison also varies how precisely the task is specified.

\subsection{Reasoning Runs and Execution Settings}

\paragraph{Comparisons between modes.}
The labels ``with reasoning'' and ``without reasoning'' refer to generation settings. Statements about individual replies refer to recorded reasoning output. Paired runs use the same model identifier and prompts, but reasoning settings, serving software, readout, retry policy, and access to calculation tools vary across the runs. These comparisons concern the evaluated configurations and do not identify a unique internal mechanism.

\paragraph{Shared evaluation prompts.}
Most configurations in Figure~\ref{fig:llm} are evaluated on the same $7{,}800$ prompts from the first $200$ tasks. These include no-description prompts at $n=1$, $2$, $4$, $\ldots$, $64$, with additional points at $n=40$ and $48$ near the $c=36$ crossing, and every description condition at $n=1$, $2$, and $4$. The gpt-oss-20b and Olmo~3 7B Think \citep{olmo2025three} runs omit the two additional baseline points and contain $7{,}400$ prompts. Results without reasoning for the open-weight models are taken from the full run restricted to these tasks. Paired mode comparisons use the same prompts, although serving environments differ as detailed below. At least $99.7\%$ of replies in each run contain a number.

\paragraph{Open-weight reasoning models.}
With reasoning, gpt-oss-20b runs in vLLM using greedy decoding, low reasoning effort, and at most $4{,}096$ new tokens. It reaches the final channel in $99.9\%$ of replies and uses $322$ tokens on average. Qwen3.5 9B runs through OpenRouter at temperature $0$, with reasoning enabled and a limit of $24{,}576$ new tokens. We pin requests to DeepInfra, which serves the model in bf16, because quantization varies across providers. Of its replies, $2{,}305$ repeat themselves until they reach the token limit. Gemma~4 31B (\texttt{gemma-4-31b-it}) runs through the Gemini API at temperature $0$. Olmo~3 7B Think (\path{allenai/Olmo-3-7B-Think}) runs in vLLM with greedy decoding and at most $32{,}768$ new tokens. Its long greedy outputs are sensitive to hardware: an earlier stated-precision run on a different GPU produces the same final number on only half the prompts. We therefore report one run. \paragraph{Models served through command-line agents.}

\subsection{Prediction Rules and Relevance Inference}

\paragraph{Per-response description weights.}
At $n=4$, we compute the per-response description weight $(\hat w-\bar y)/(m-\bar y)$. This quantity describes one response and differs from the fitted coefficient obtained by regressing predictions across tasks. We use the tasks with $|m-\bar y|\geq30$, $61$ at $c=36$ and $124$ at $c=1$, and exclude replies without a number. A reply is classified as consistent with a rule when its per-response weight is within $0.05$ of that rule's weight. The candidate weights are the Bayesian reference ($0.90$ for $c=36$ and $0.20$ for $c=1$); the reference with its two precisions exchanged ($0.10$ and $0.80$); the description counted as one additional observation, $1/(n+1)$; the description alone, with weight $1$; and the demonstrations alone, with weight $0$. The one-additional-observation rule coincides with the reference at $c=1$.

With reasoning, Opus~4.6, GPT-6 Sol, Gemma~4 31B, and Haiku~5.5 match the reference rule on $89$ to $100\%$ of eligible replies. Without reasoning, $77\%$ of Opus~4.6 replies at $c=36$ match none of these candidate rules, as do more than half of GPT-6 Sol replies. Olmo~3 7B Think averages demonstrations well but reaches summary ESS only $6.2$ at $c=36$. At $n=1$, it repeats the description value in $57$ to $80\%$ of replies, more often at higher stated precision. At $n=4$, about half of its eligible replies return the description value alone and a third match the reference. Without a description, its root-mean-square error falls from $56$ at $n=1$ to $10.8$ at $n=32$, compared with $10.0$ for the reference. Its shortfall therefore lies in combining the description with demonstrations. Enabling reasoning does not ensure agreement with the reference in every model.

\subsection{Stated Reliability}\label{app:llm-reliability}

We test stated reliability in two designs. In the matched design of Section~\ref{sec:llm-stated}, descriptions are irrelevant with the stated probability and the prompt states what holds in that case. In an earlier design, every description remained relevant and the warning left the irrelevant case open; we report it second, because it shows what models do when the prompt does not determine how to update.

\paragraph{Matched design.}
The prompt adds the sentence ``There is a 10\% chance that this statement is wrong, in which case the mean was instead drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 100 to 999,'' or the same sentence with 50\%. Each of the $200$ tasks is evaluated with its relevant $c=36$ description and with an irrelevant one, whose center is drawn independently of the task from the same marginal distribution. With stated chance $1-p$, the error with the description is $p$ times the mean squared error with the relevant description plus $1-p$ times that with the irrelevant one, so that the stated chance is the true one. ESS is then computed as in the main runs, against each model's own baseline curve, with intervals that resample tasks. \emph{Fixed-weight averaging} conditions each component on the demonstrations and averages the two posterior means with the prior weights $p$ and $1-p$; a predictor that ignores the warning treats every description as relevant.

Table~\ref{tab:matched} reports the results. At a $10\%$ chance, the reference's summary ESS is $5.0$, against $34.6$ without the warning, and the two simpler predictors have ESS $0.3$ and $-0.1$: the description's remaining value depends on recognizing, from the demonstrations, the tasks on which it is irrelevant. At a $50\%$ chance, the reference's ESS is $0.9$, and both simpler predictors do worse with the description than without it. GPT-6 Sol with reasoning is within $0.2$ of the reference at both chances, and about nine in ten of its replies are within $2$ of the reference's prediction with either kind of description. Haiku~5.5 falls short, at a $10\%$ chance mainly with irrelevant descriptions and at $50\%$ with both kinds. Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol gains little or nothing from the description.

\paragraph{When the prompt leaves the irrelevant case open.}
In the earlier design, the warning gave a $10\%$ or $50\%$ chance that the description is ``wrong,'' in which case ``the mean is unknown,'' and the descriptions themselves remained relevant. A stated $50\%$ chance of irrelevance then reduces reference ESS from $34.6$ to $5.7$ under the uniform baseline prior (Table~\ref{tab:reason}). Without reasoning, ESS falls by $36$ to $62\%$ for four models, against $84\%$ for the reference; GPT-6 Sol's falls from $10.3$ to $0.9$.

\paragraph{Stating the baseline prior.}
Computing $\pi_n$ requires the marginal likelihood of the demonstrations under each component, and hence the baseline prior under irrelevance. The original warning says only that the mean is otherwise ``unknown,'' so the prompt does not determine a unique posterior relevance probability: a model must supply the prior from its pretrained knowledge, and the reference must assume one. The assumption matters: with its baseline curve held fixed, the reference's summary ESS at the stated $50\%$ is $5.7$ under our uniform prior over $100$ to $999$, $4.6$ under the generating distribution $\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$, and $13.4$ under a uniform prior over $0$ to $2000$. To remove this ambiguity, we rerun the stated-reliability condition on the same $200$ tasks, at $c=36$, stated irrelevance probabilities of $10\%$ and $50\%$, and $n=1$, $2$, and $4$. The prompt now states the reference's uniform baseline prior over $100$ to $999$. We use GPT-6 Sol at low effort and Haiku~5.5 at medium effort, with $1{,}200$ prompts each; these runs are also the relevant descriptions of the matched design above. Table~\ref{tab:fallback} compares their fitted description weights with those from the original runs.

At the stated $50\%$, the reference and fixed-weight predictions differ by at least $5$ on $123$ to $155$ tasks per cell. On these tasks, the share of replies closer to the reference than to the fixed-weight prediction rises from $0$--$5\%$ to $92$--$97\%$ for GPT-6 Sol, and from $21$--$41\%$ to $87$--$95\%$ for Haiku~5.5. At the stated $10\%$, at most $7$ tasks per cell separate the two rules by this amount, so the comparison is uninformative there. Pooling the three values of $n$ at the stated $50\%$, the share of replies within $2$ of the reference prediction rises from $10\%$ to $90\%$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $23\%$ to $58\%$ for Haiku~5.5.

A constant increase in the description weight could also make replies closer to the reference, because its posterior relevance probability exceeds the stated $50\%$ on most tasks. To test whether the weights vary with the evidence, we use each reply's \emph{normalized description weight}: its per-response weight divided by $c/(c+n)$, restricting to tasks with $|m-\bar y|\geq30$. For the scalar reference, this equals its posterior relevance probability $\pi_n$, up to the negligible boundary effects discussed above. For model predictions, the ratio can lie outside $[0,1]$ and does not identify an internal probability. Here $\pi_n$ ranges from $0.08$ to $0.88$ as the demonstrations provide different evidence for relevance; fixed-weight averaging keeps the normalized weight constant.

We regress the models' normalized weights on $\pi_n$, pooling $n=1$, $2$, and $4$ at the stated $50\%$ ($269$ replies per model). With the baseline prior stated, the slopes are $0.96$ [$0.84$, $1.04$] for GPT-6 Sol and $0.98$ [$0.82$, $1.15$] for Haiku~5.5, compared with $0.01$ [$-0.09$, $0.11$] and $0.22$ [$-0.01$, $0.45$] under the original warning. The $95\%$ intervals resample the $153$ tasks. The corresponding intercepts with the prior stated are $0.01$ and $0.03$, and $R^2$ is $0.57$ and $0.33$.

The agreement also holds when the demonstrations count against the description. Among the $23$ replies with $\pi_n$ below $0.5$ (mean $0.33$), the mean normalized weights are $0.30$ and $0.38$ for the two models with the baseline prior stated, compared with $0.48$ and $0.50$ under the original warning. Both models therefore vary their description weights across tasks in approximately the amount predicted by Bayesian model averaging.

Summary ESS uses each model's baseline curve from the main run. At the stated $50\%$, fixed-weight averaging gives $4.4$ and the Bayesian reference gives $5.7$. Stating the baseline prior raises ESS from $4.2$ to $5.4$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $3.8$ to $4.2$ for Haiku~5.5. At the stated $10\%$, it changes from $29.7$ to $25.0$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $32.6$ to $15.1$ for Haiku~5.5, compared with $27.5$ for the reference. The two models' fitted weights agree within $0.01$ in this condition (Table~\ref{tab:fallback}). Haiku's lower ESS therefore does not reflect lower average reliance on the description; it comes from a minority of replies far from the reference, some of which repeat a demonstration value. Two further configurations were run with the baseline prior stated. Haiku~5.5 at low effort also updates (slope $0.93$), with summary ESS $2.7$ at the stated $50\%$, against $5.5$ under the original warning. Opus~4.6 without reasoning does not (slope $-0.18$), with summary ESS $3.8$ under both warnings.

\subsection{Wording with Reasoning Enabled}

Because reasoning brings description weights close to the reference under the original wording, we test whether it does so under the alternative wordings as well. We evaluate the three alternative wordings with Haiku~5.5 at medium effort, for the three relevant descriptions at $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$ on the first $200$ tasks; all $5{,}400$ replies contain a number. With ``An earlier estimate put its mean at $m$, with a standard error of $\tau$'', at least $71\%$ of replies in each cell include reasoning output; $99\%$ of these lie within $1$ of the reference prediction. Under ``Its mean is about $m$, give or take $\tau$'' and ``Mean: $m \pm \tau$'', reasoning output is absent from $4$ to $36\%$ and $2$ to $74\%$ of replies per cell, respectively. Even among replies with reasoning output, $71\%$ and $91\%$ repeat the description value, while $31\%$ and $20\%$ lie within $1$ of the reference prediction. At $c = 1$ and $n = 4$, its description weights are $0.72$ and $0.93$, compared with reference weight $0.20$.

All wordings provide a value $m$ and a scale $\tau$, but ``give or take'' and ``$\pm$'' do not identify $\tau$ as a prior standard deviation or specify a distribution for the mean. The comparison varies the explicitness of the probabilistic specification as well as its wording. The results show that accurate combination of descriptions and demonstrations under explicit statistical statements need not extend to these less explicit formulations.

\subsection{A Semantic Task}\label{app:semantic}

The numerical descriptions state a distribution over the task, whereas descriptions in practice usually state constraints in ordinary language that the model must interpret. We therefore design a message-filing task with such descriptions that still admits an exact Bayes-optimal predictor. As in Bayesian models of rule-based concept learning \citep{goodman2008}, the hidden task is one of a finite set of rules, here $60$ filing policies under a uniform prior. A policy note constrains the policy in ordinary language: a broad note leaves $20$ compatible policies, and a specific note leaves $2$. With reasoning, Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6 Sol obtain ESS $9.9$ and $9.5$ for the specific note at $n=2$, compared with $11.4$ for the reference, and when the note may be irrelevant, most of their predictions are closer to the Bayesian reference than to fixed-weight averaging.

\paragraph{Task.}
A message has six binary properties: its sender is outside the organization; it requests a payment; it concerns travel; it requests computer access; it has a deadline within a day; and it contains personal contact information. Each message states every property as present or absent, using one of three phrasings for each value, such as ``I'm a contractor from another company.'' or ``I'm on the sales team here.'' Properties are sampled independently with probability one half, phrasings are sampled uniformly, and sentences appear in random order.

A policy uses exactly two properties and requires each to be present or absent. A message goes into folder A when both requirements hold, and into B otherwise. There are $\binom{6}{2}2^2=60$ such policies. The prompt describes this family and states that the company's policy was chosen uniformly from it. It then provides a policy note, $n$ previously filed messages with their folders, and four new messages. The model is asked for each new message's probability of going into A, as a number between $0$ and $1$, on four lines from ``1: \textless probability\textgreater'' to ``4: \textless probability\textgreater''. Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol identifies each property correctly in $97$--$100\%$ of $96$ messages; all three misreadings concern one phrasing, ``I'm not traveling for this.''

\paragraph{Policy notes.}
A broad note reads, for example, ``Policy note: whether it concerns travel is one of the two properties the policy uses,'' leaving $20$ possible policies. A specific note reads ``Policy note: to go into A, a message must request a payment. The other property the policy uses is whether it concerns travel,'' leaving $2$. These notes communicate constraints in ordinary language; the model must determine which policies satisfy them.

The unreliable note adds ``This note has a 50\% chance of being wrong, in which case it says nothing about the policy, which is then any of the 60, equally likely.'' In the generator, the note is relevant with probability one half and otherwise describes an independently drawn policy. The irrelevant component occurs in $92$ of the $200$ tasks. An independently generated note can agree with the actual policy by chance. Thus, \emph{relevance} denotes the latent generating component, whereas \emph{compatibility} denotes whether a policy satisfies the note; the two are not interchangeable.

\paragraph{Reference and ESS.}
For a note asserted to be relevant, the reference predicts the fraction of policies consistent with the note and the filed messages that assign the new message to A. For an unreliable note, it averages this prediction with the prediction from policies consistent with the filed messages alone, weighting the two by the posterior relevance probability. The prior relevance probability is $0.5$; the posterior probability is obtained using the likelihood of the demonstrations under each component. On $3{,}000$ simulated tasks, the reference's predictive probabilities are calibrated in every condition, and its posterior probability that the unreliable note is relevant matches the frequency of relevance: where it exceeds $0.7$, it averages $0.80$, and the note is relevant in $80\%$ of cases.

The loss is the squared error between the predicted probability of A and the true folder indicator, averaged over the four query messages. ESS follows Definition~\ref{def:ess}, using each learner's baseline curve, its loss without a note, at $n=0$, $2$, $4$, $6$, $8$, $12$, $16$, $20$, $24$, and $32$, with linear interpolation in $n$. On simulated tasks, this grid changes the reference ESS by at most $0.6$ relative to evaluation at every integer demonstration count.

\paragraph{Runs.}
We use $200$ tasks, each defined by a sampled policy, and $19$ conditions: no note at the ten demonstration counts above, and each note at $n=0$, $2$, and $4$. This gives $3{,}800$ prompts per configuration. Haiku~5.5 runs at medium effort; GPT-6 Sol runs without reasoning and at low effort. The command-line agents follow Appendix~\ref{app:llm}, with no tools, and no reply calls a tool. Fourteen replies from GPT-6 Sol at low effort contain no probability and are excluded; one Haiku~5.5 reply omits one of its four probabilities. Every Haiku~5.5 reply includes reasoning output, with a median of $3{,}008$ reasoning tokens. For GPT-6 Sol at low effort, $91\%$ of replies include reasoning output, with a median of $142$ reasoning tokens.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:semantic} reports each note's ESS. With reasoning, both models are within $2$ demonstrations of the reference in every condition except GPT-6 Sol's specific note at $n=4$: its ESS is $26.1$, compared with $14.8$ for the reference. Its loss with this note, $0.008$, is close to the reference's $0.007$, but its baseline curve flattens beyond $n=16$, with loss $0.022$ at $n=20$, compared with $0.004$ for the reference. It consequently needs more demonstrations on its own baseline curve to match the note's error reduction.

For the unreliable note, we compare predictions with the Bayesian reference and with fixed-weight averaging, which averages the two component predictions using their prior weights. Here it retains the stated $50\%$ weight unless the filed messages rule out every policy compatible with the note; in that case, it assigns zero weight to the note-conditioned component. We use query messages on which the two predictions differ by at least $0.05$: about $150$ at $n=2$ and $200$ at $n=4$, out of $800$. The share of predictions closer to the reference is $90\%$ at $n=2$ and $99\%$ at $n=4$ for Haiku~5.5; $77\%$ and $87\%$ for GPT-6 Sol at low effort; and $45\%$ at both counts for GPT-6 Sol without reasoning.

Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol learns slowly from demonstrations. Its loss without a note is still $0.078$ at $n=32$, compared with $0.000$ for the reference at the reported precision. Measured against this weaker baseline, a note can have an ESS as large as the reference's or larger. For example, the specific note has ESS $13.5$ at $n=2$, although the model's loss with that note is $0.130$, compared with $0.027$ for the reference. This illustrates why learner-specific ESS must be interpreted alongside absolute prediction error.

\section{Additional Related Work}\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Task descriptions and demonstrations.}
\citet{weller2020} formalize learning from task descriptions by distinguishing the latent task, its natural-language description, and individual input--output examples. We use \emph{task description} for information about the task supplied in the prompt, including a numeric description token in the meta-trained setting and probabilistic statements or policy constraints in the language-model experiments. A \emph{demonstration} is an observed input--output example; in scalar mean estimation, it is an observation at the fixed input $x=1$. Summaries distilled from demonstrations can serve as descriptions \citep{honda2025}; our controlled experiments instead specify the joint distribution of task, description, and demonstrations.

\paragraph{Prior information and uncertain relevance.}
The two-component construction is related to robust mixture priors, which combine informative and baseline distributions and update their component weights after observing data \citep{schmidli2014}. Our mixture is specified directly, rather than derived from a historical-data meta-analysis. Reliability $p$ is the prior probability of the description-conditioned component; $\pi_n$ is its posterior probability. Averaging component predictions using $\pi_n$ is Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999}. In a robust mixture prior, the prior weight is fixed but the posterior weights still update; fixed-weight averaging, our experimental comparison, omits this update.

In our model, an irrelevant description is independent of the task and may happen to agree with it. Irrelevance is therefore a property of how the description was generated, whereas prior--data conflict concerns disagreement between prior information and observed data.

\paragraph{Specificity and description weights.}
Specificity takes a concrete form in each setting: the prior precision $c$ in the Gaussian model, together with the number $k$ of constrained directions for a partial description, and the number of compatible policies in the semantic experiment. These quantities do not share a scale across tasks; ESS expresses each in demonstrations. Our description weights follow Gaussian mean estimation, in which the posterior combines the prior mean and the sample mean with weights proportional to their precisions \citep{neuenschwander2020}. Fitted and per-response weights summarize model predictions; their normalized values can be compared with posterior component probabilities but need not be probabilities.

\paragraph{Effective sample size and predictive comparisons.}
Prior ESS has several definitions, including matching local information \citep{morita2008}, matching expected posterior loss \citep{reimherr2021}, and quantifying sample savings at a target estimation accuracy \citep{wiesenfarth2020}. Matching information requires an explicit prior. A network or a language model brings prior knowledge from training, but that knowledge is implicit in its weights, whereas its prediction error can be measured with and without the description. Our definition therefore follows the risk-matching approach, but it evaluates population prediction error, allows a non-Bayesian learner, and depends on the demonstration count. The word \emph{predictive} specifies what is matched; it does not assert the additive predictive-consistency property studied by \citet{neuenschwander2020}.

Two predictive comparisons are especially close. \citet{lescao2021} average horizontal data advantages over an accuracy range in prompt-based fine-tuning; we evaluate a pointwise difference in demonstration counts. \citet{selby2025} compare priors elicited from language models with data-based predictors by the observations needed to match predictive loss; we study descriptions supplied to a learner and how their benefit changes after demonstrations. Beyond ICL, an instruction can replace up to $100$ execution examples in the fine-tuning settings studied by \citet{cook2026}. \citet{wenliang2026} compare optimized prompts with demonstrations in terms of their length and effect.

\paragraph{Demonstrations, text, and probabilistic prediction.}
Other work examines semantic priors and flipped labels \citep{wei2023}, weak dependence on demonstration labels \citep{min2022}, Bayesian scaling laws \citep{arora2025}, and Bayesian reference curves for in-context function learning \citep{akata2026}. Text supplied alongside numbers can change numerical predictions \citep{requeima2024}. Related work studies how language-model outputs compare with Bayesian inference and how training can improve probabilistic reasoning \citep{qiu2026}; \citet{he2025} examine Bayesian consistency during iterative reasoning on open-ended questions. Our numerical experiments state uncertainty explicitly, allowing comparison with a specified Bayesian reference as precision and relevance probability vary. The semantic task extends this comparison to ordinary-language constraints on a finite family of classification policies.
