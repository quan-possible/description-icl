\begin{abstract}
Prompts for in-context learning (ICL) often describe a task as well as demonstrate it through examples. Yet how descriptions work, especially in relation to demonstrations, remains unclear. Through the Bayesian view of ICL, we characterize descriptions as informative priors by their \emph{specificity} and \emph{reliability}, and borrow the effective sample size (ESS) from Bayesian statistics to measure their worth in demonstrations. Our theoretical results on in-context linear regression tasks show that a reliable description's ESS grows with its specificity and with the task's dimension. However, the ESS drastically falls if the description may be irrelevant: a one-in-ten chance of irrelevance cuts a precise description's ESS from 117 to 13.5. We then translate the setting into experiments on Transformer networks and pretrained language models, including frontier ones. Meta-trained Transformers come close to the Bayes-optimal ESS and adapt to unreliable descriptions when trained on them. Pretrained language models, however, surprisingly show little regard for descriptions when answering without reasoning, and closely match the Bayesian reference only when reasoning is enabled. This effect is mostly robust to different levels of model capability.

% What limits a description, however, is whether it can be trusted: in our setting, a precise description is worth 117 demonstrations, but a one-in-ten chance that it is irrelevant cuts this to 13.5, and demonstrations are then needed to check it. Learners also differ in how much of this worth they realize. Meta-trained Transformers come close to the Bayes-optimal ESS and inherit their trust in descriptions from training. Without reasoning, pretrained language models realize little of a precise description's worth, 2 to 10 demonstrations against 35 for a Bayesian reference; with reasoning, most come close to the reference. Two models we retest also track Bayesian inference about relevance, but only once the prompt states what irrelevance means. What a description is worth thus depends on the learner as well as on the description.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models are capable of in-context learning (ICL): given a prompt that specifies a task through a natural-language description, demonstrations, or both, they can predict the output for a new query input \citep{brown2020}. In practice, prompts consist mostly of system instructions, user instructions, skill files, and earlier conversation turns, which typically describe the task rather than demonstrate it \citep{mao2025,patsakis2026}. The balance shifts further as LLMs carry out increasingly long-horizon tasks \citep{kwa2025}, for which demonstrations are costly to provide.

Theories of ICL, however, have mostly focused on prompts made of demonstrations alone \citep{xie2022,garg2022,akyurek2023}. The few that include descriptions study only specific types, such as a descriptor that describes the input distribution \citep{huangge2025}, a set of candidate hypotheses \citep{lin2025}, or related datasets supplied as context \citep{zhu2026}; none offers a systematic treatment of descriptions in general. We therefore ask: how can we characterize natural-language descriptions to formally understand and analyze them? And how do descriptions compare, combine, and interact with demonstrations?

To address these questions, we study $d$-dimensional Gaussian linear regression, a standard controlled setting for ICL \citep{garg2022,panwar2024}. A description supplies information about the regression coefficients before demonstrations are observed. A mixture model separates two kinds of uncertainty: conditional on relevance, the description specifies a Gaussian distribution with precision $c$; with probability $1-p$, it is independent of the task. We thus characterize a description by two quantities: its \emph{specificity}, set by $c$, and its \emph{reliability}, the prior probability $p$ that it is relevant.

To compare descriptions with demonstrations, we adapt sample-size measures of prior impact and predictive performance \citep{wiesenfarth2020,reimherr2021,lescao2021,selby2025}. For a given learner and prediction loss, a description's \emph{predictive effective sample size} (ESS) at $n$ demonstrations is the additional number of demonstrations needed without the description to reach the same expected error. Computing ESS for both the Bayes-optimal predictor and trained models distinguishes the information available under the statistical model from the benefit realized by a learner.

We test the analysis with Transformers trained from scratch on the Gaussian model and with pretrained language models prompted on numerical mean-estimation and semantic classification tasks. The Transformers learn a description token's precision and reliability from the training distribution. The language models receive probabilistic descriptions and policy constraints in text.

Our main findings are as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Precision and dimension determine the value of reliable descriptions.} With fixed positive integer precision $c$, zero-demonstration ESS approaches $c+d+1$ as the baseline prior becomes diffuse. A partial description can become more valuable as demonstrations constrain the directions it leaves unspecified (Section~\ref{sec:specificity}).
\item \textbf{Uncertain relevance limits zero-demonstration ESS.} Any fixed positive probability of irrelevance imposes a finite ceiling as precision grows. For $d=16$, baseline variance $a_0=10$, and $c=100$, reducing reliability from $1$ to $0.9$ reduces ESS from approximately $117$ to $13.5$. Demonstrations can provide evidence about relevance (Section~\ref{sec:reliability}).
\item \textbf{Description use depends on training and prompt specifications.} Meta-trained Transformers approach the Bayes-optimal ESS, while their response to reliability shifts reflects training. For most pretrained language models tested, enabling reasoning improves the use of stated precision. For two models, explicitly specifying the irrelevant-component distribution also produces predictions consistent with Bayesian model averaging in numerical and semantic tasks (Sections~\ref{sec:networks}--\ref{sec:llm}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Framework}\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Tasks and demonstrations.}
A task is a vector $w\in\R^d$ with baseline prior $\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d)$, where $a_0>0$. A prompt contains $n$ demonstrations $D_n=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ and a query input $x$, with
\[
x_i,x\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,I_d),\qquad
y_i=w^\top x_i+\epsilon_i,\quad
\epsilon_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1).
\]
Inputs and observation noise are independent of the task and one another. Numerical illustrations in Sections~\ref{sec:specificity}--\ref{sec:reliability} use $d=16$ and $a_0=10$.

\paragraph{Descriptions and relevance.}
A description supplies a center $m\in\R^d$. Its \emph{specificity} is represented by precision $c\geq a_0^{-1}$, and its \emph{reliability} is $p\in[0,1]$. Let $r=(ca_0)^{-1}\in(0,1]$ be the fraction of baseline variance remaining in the relevant component. With a latent relevance indicator $Z\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p)$ independent of $m$, the joint model is
\begin{align*}
m&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,(1-r)a_0I_d),\\
w\mid m,Z=1&\sim\mathcal{N}(m,ra_0I_d),\\
w\mid m,Z=0&\sim\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d).
\end{align*}
Thus
\[
w\mid m\sim p\,\mathcal{N}(m,c^{-1}I_d)
 +(1-p)\,\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d).
\]
This two-component structure is used in robust mixture priors \citep{schmidli2014}. Integrating over $m$ recovers $w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,a_0I_d)$ under either component, so comparisons with and without descriptions concern the same task population.

A description is \emph{relevant} when $Z=1$ and \emph{irrelevant} when $Z=0$. Relevance identifies the generating component, irrespective of the realized distance between $m$ and $w$. We use \emph{reliable} for $p=1$ and \emph{unreliable} for $p<1$. At $c=a_0^{-1}$, the two components coincide and the description provides no task information.

\paragraph{Prediction error.}
For a prediction $\hat y$, we measure expected squared error against the noise-free query response:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:regret}
R=\E\big[(\hat y-w^\top x)^2\big].
\end{equation}
We call this quantity \emph{prediction error}, or \emph{risk}; it excludes irreducible query noise. The expectation is over tasks, descriptions, demonstrations, and queries. For a fixed learner, $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)$ and $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)$ denote risk with and without a description, and $R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)$ denotes the reliable case.

\begin{definition}[Predictive effective sample size]\label{def:ess}
Given $n$ demonstrations, let $n^*$ be the smallest $t\geq0$ satisfying $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)=R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)$. When this crossing exists, the description's ESS is
\[
\ess_n=n^*-n.
\]
Noninteger counts refer to an interpolated baseline curve.
\end{definition}

We interpolate risk linearly in demonstration count for the theory, meta-trained Transformers, and semantic task, and linearly in log count for numerical language-model tasks. Empirical ESS uses the first crossing in the evaluated range. When no crossing is observed, we report censoring at the corresponding boundary under the conventions in Appendix~\ref{app:llm}; an exact ESS is then unavailable.

An ESS of $10$ means that $n$ demonstrations plus the description match the error of $n+10$ demonstrations alone. For a decreasing baseline curve, negative ESS indicates higher error at the same demonstration count. Because each learner uses its own baseline, a large ESS need not imply low absolute error. For the Bayes-optimal predictor under the correctly specified model, conditioning on descriptions cannot increase expected risk, so ESS is nonnegative.

\section{The Value of a Reliable Description}\label{sec:specificity}

For $p=1$, a description with fixed positive integer precision $c$ has $\ess_0\to c+d+1$ as $a_0\to\infty$. Its value without demonstrations therefore depends on task dimension as well as precision. Write $W_n=X_n^\top X_n$, where the rows of $X_n$ are the demonstration inputs. Gaussian conjugacy gives the Bayes-optimal risks
\begin{align*}
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)&=\E\operatorname{tr}(a_0^{-1}I_d+W_n)^{-1},\\
R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)&=\E\operatorname{tr}(cI_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\end{align*}

\begin{proposition}[Reliable descriptions]\label{prop:reliable}
Let $p=1$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item With $r\in(0,1]$ fixed, $\ess_0\geq(1-r)d$, and $\ess_0\to(1-r)d$ as $a_0\to\infty$.
\item With positive integer $c$ fixed, $\ess_0\to c+d+1$ as $a_0\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The fixed-precision limit in part (ii) follows by comparing the description's risk $d/c$ with the limiting demonstrations-only risk $d/(n-d-1)$, valid for integer $n>d+1$ \citep{muirhead1982}. The risks match at $n=c+d+1$. Integer $c$ places this crossing on the grid used to interpolate the learning curve. In this limit, demonstrations-only risk depends on $\E\operatorname{tr}(W_n^{-1})=d/(n-d-1)$, whereas $\E W_n=nI_d$; this difference accounts for the additional $d+1$ demonstrations.

Part (i) describes a different scaling. Holding $r$ fixed makes $c=(ra_0)^{-1}$ decrease as $a_0$ grows, so the description removes a fixed fraction $1-r$ of baseline variance. Before the demonstration inputs span $\R^d$, error in the remaining directions dominates as $a_0\to\infty$. The description then replaces $(1-r)d$ demonstrations in the limit, with the same quantity providing a lower bound at finite $a_0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:specificity}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/ess_sq.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Reliability limits the benefit of specificity without demonstrations.} Bayes-optimal $\ess_0$ versus precision $c$, for $p=1$ and $p=0.9$, with $d=16$ and $a_0=10$. The dotted line is the finite ceiling as $c\to\infty$ at $p=0.9$. Estimates use eight batches of $4{,}000$ prompts; standard errors are below $0.02$ demonstrations.}
\label{fig:ess}
\end{figure}

At $d=16$ and $a_0=10$, a reliable description with $c=1$ has ESS $15.6$, compared with the lower bound $(1-r)d=14.4$. Increasing $c$ to $100$ raises ESS to approximately $117$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ess}). With many demonstrations, the dimension-dependent contribution disappears: $\ess_n\to c-a_0^{-1}$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}). After $100$ demonstrations, the $c=1$ description has ESS $1.1$, close to its added precision of $0.9$.

\paragraph{The number of constrained directions matters.}
A reliable description may constrain only $k<d$ directions. With fixed $c>0$, its zero-demonstration ESS approaches $k$ as $a_0\to\infty$: the unconstrained directions dominate the remaining error. With many demonstrations, its ESS instead approaches $k(c-a_0^{-1})/d$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}, Appendix~\ref{app:partial}). A sufficiently precise partial description can therefore gain value as demonstrations resolve what it leaves unspecified. At $d=16$, $k=8$, and $c=100$, ESS rises from $8.1$ without demonstrations to $36.2$ after $100$ demonstrations (Table~\ref{tab:partial}).

\section{The Cost of Unreliability}\label{sec:reliability}

Uncertain relevance limits the benefit of increasing precision. For every fixed $p<1$, the zero-demonstration risk remains positive as $c\to\infty$, giving a finite ESS ceiling.

For $0<p<1$, let $\mu_z=\E[w\mid m,D_n,Z=z]$ be the component posterior means and let $\pi_n=P(Z=1\mid m,D_n)$ be the \emph{posterior relevance probability}. Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999} gives
\[
\hat y=\big[\pi_n\mu_1+(1-\pi_n)\mu_0\big]^\top x.
\]
The law of total variance then yields the following risk decomposition.

\begin{proposition}[Risk under uncertain relevance]\label{prop:unreliable}
For every $n\geq0$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
\begin{split}
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)={}&pR^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)\\
&+(1-p)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)+\mathcal{V}_n,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where
\[
\mathcal{V}_n=\E\!\left[\pi_n(1-\pi_n)
\big((\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top x\big)^2\right]\geq0.
\]
Without demonstrations,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zero}
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(0)=\big[1-p^2(1-r)\big]a_0d.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

Here $\mathcal{V}_n$ is the expected between-component posterior variance. The first two terms in Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp} are the risk of the Bayes-optimal predictor that also observes $Z$, so $\mathcal{V}_n$ is the excess Bayes risk due to unobserved relevance. Without demonstrations, observing $Z$ would remove a fraction $p(1-r)$ of baseline risk; without $Z$, the reduction is only $p^2(1-r)$ (Appendix~\ref{app:reliability}).

\begin{corollary}[ESS under uncertain relevance]\label{cor:ess}
\leavevmode
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*),nosep,leftmargin=*]
\item For $0<p<1$, define $n_p^{\sharp}$ by
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n_p^{\sharp})=(1-p^2)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0).
\]
Then $\ess_0<n_p^{\sharp}$ at every finite $c$, and $\ess_0\to n_p^{\sharp}$ as $c\to\infty$. As $a_0\to\infty$ with $r$ fixed, $\ess_0\to p^2(1-r)d$ and $n_p^{\sharp}\to p^2d$. For $p=0$, both ESS and the ceiling are zero.
\item With $d$, $a_0$, and $c$ fixed, as $n\to\infty$,
\[
\begin{cases}
\ess_n\to c-a_0^{-1},&p=1,\\
\limsup\limits_{n\to\infty}\ess_n\leq p(c-a_0^{-1}),&p<1.
\end{cases}
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Uncertain relevance imposes an error floor without demonstrations.}
For fixed $p<1$, Equation~\eqref{eq:zero} approaches the positive floor $(1-p^2)a_0d$ as $c\to\infty$, giving a finite ceiling on zero-demonstration ESS. At $p=0.9$, $d=16$, and $a_0=10$, increasing $c$ from $1$ to $100$ raises ESS only from $12.0$ to $13.5$, compared with $15.6$ to $117$ at $p=1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:ess}). The finite-$a_0$ ceiling is approximately $13.5$; its limit as the baseline prior becomes diffuse is $p^2d=12.96$.

\paragraph{Demonstrations distinguish low precision from uncertain relevance.}
Without demonstrations, an unreliable description has the same risk as a reliable description with remaining variance fraction $1-p^2(1-r)$. For example, $c=100$, $p=0.9$ and a reliable description with $c\approx0.52$ both give ESS about $13.5$ here. After $100$ demonstrations, their ESS values are $86.6$ and $0.5$. Demonstrations can support the high-precision component of the unreliable description; the reliable low-precision description has no such component. A predictor that assumes relevance by fixing $p=1$ can instead increase error (Appendix~\ref{app:misspecification}).
\section{Meta-Trained Transformers}\label{sec:networks}

We first test whether Transformers learn to use descriptions under the correctly specified model. Following \citet{garg2022}, we train from scratch with $d=5$ and $a_0=10$. Prompts contain an optional description token carrying $m$, up to $20$ demonstration tokens, and a query token \citep{huangge2025}. The description token does not state $c$ or $p$; the network must learn these properties across training tasks. Half the prompts omit the description, supplying a demonstrations-only baseline for each network. We train separate networks for $c\in\{1,10\}$ and $p\in\{1,0.99,0.9\}$, with two or three seeds per configuration (Appendix~\ref{app:networks}).

\paragraph{Matched training and test distributions.}
At $n=0$, networks trained with $c=10$, $p=1$ achieve mean ESS $14.3$ (Figure~\ref{fig:networks}a), compared with Bayes-optimal ESS $15.9$ estimated on a larger sample (Table~\ref{tab:networks}). At lower reliability, and for $c=1$, zero-demonstration ESS is within $0.3$ of the Bayes-optimal value. Networks also learn the complementarity of partial descriptions and demonstrations. With $k=3$ of $d=5$ directions described at $c=10$, ESS rises from $3.27$ at $n=0$ to $5.55$ at $n=10$, compared with Bayes-optimal values $3.22$ and $5.64$.

\paragraph{Reliability shifts.}
We evaluate networks at test reliabilities different from their training reliability. We compare the Bayesian predictor using the training reliability, $p_{\mathrm{train}}$, with the Bayes-optimal predictor using the test reliability, $p_{\mathrm{test}}$ \citep{raventos2023}. At $n=0$, the Bayesian prediction is $p\,m^\top x$. We summarize a network's predictions by the \emph{fitted description weight} $q$, estimated by regressing them on $m^\top x$. This coefficient describes prediction behavior and need not represent a probability inside the network.

For $c=10$, networks trained at $p=0.9$ retain weights near $0.9$ even when every test description is relevant (Figure~\ref{fig:networks}b). This is consistent with the information available: $m$ alone does not reveal the changed test reliability. When $10\%$ of test descriptions are irrelevant, networks trained at $p=1$ have higher error with a description than without one from six demonstrations onward, in every seed. Training at $p=0.99$ avoids this failure across the tested range. Exposure to occasional irrelevance changes how the networks use subsequent evidence.

\section{Pretrained Language Models}\label{sec:llm}

We next ask whether pretrained language models use description statistics stated in a prompt. We study numerical mean estimation, a scalar regression task with fixed input $x=1$, followed by semantic classification. The scalar task tests the use of precision and relevance probabilities without the random-design dimension effect.

\subsection{Task and Evaluation}

Each numerical task has a hidden mean $w\sim\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$ and demonstrations drawn from $\mathcal{N}(w,\sigma^2)$, with $\sigma=60$, then rounded to integers. A description gives a center $m$ and standard deviation $\tau$: “Its mean was drawn from a normal distribution with mean 510 and standard deviation 30.” Before rounding and range restrictions, the relevant component satisfies $w\mid m\sim\mathcal{N}(m,\tau^2)$. We measure description precision relative to one observation, $c=\sigma^2/\tau^2$, retaining the unit-noise convention of the theory. Stated standard deviations $60$, $30$, and $10$ correspond to $c=1$, $4$, and $36$.

We vary stated precision and add warnings of a $50\%$ chance that the description is “wrong.” These warnings change the stated reliability while the descriptions remain relevant. In a separate fixed-value control, we hold $m$ and all demonstrations fixed and describe a center generated at $c=36$ as having $c=4$. This isolates the response to stated precision. Appendix~\ref{app:llm} also evaluates irrelevant descriptions and actual mixtures of the two relevance branches.

The main comparison uses the same $200$ tasks for five models with reasoning enabled and disabled: gpt-oss-20b \citep{openai2025}, Qwen3.5 9B \citep{qwen35}, Gemma~4 31B \citep{gemma4}, GPT-6 Sol \citep{openai2026sol}, and Claude Opus~4.6 \citep{anthropic2026opus}. Claude Haiku~5.5 \citep{anthropic2026haiku} is evaluated with reasoning at medium effort. “Reasoning enabled” denotes the configured reasoning mode; individual replies may still contain no recorded reasoning output. Paired runs use the same model identifier, although serving and decoding settings differ for some models. Appendix~\ref{app:llm} gives execution and readout details and a larger run without reasoning on $2{,}000$ tasks.

\paragraph{Bayesian reference.}
Our \emph{prompt-based Bayesian reference} uses the observation noise and description uncertainty stated in the prompt. When the mean is described as unknown, it assumes a uniform prior over the integers $100$ to $999$, both without a description and under irrelevance. When the prompt asserts relevance, its prediction is approximately the precision-weighted Gaussian posterior mean \citep{neuenschwander2020},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-rule}
\hat w_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{c\,m+n\bar y}{c+n},
\end{equation}
where $\bar y$ is the demonstration mean. Rounding and boundary effects are negligible for this expression. In the idealized continuous Gaussian model with a flat baseline prior, a correctly specified description has exact ESS $c$. Every observation measures the same scalar mean, so there is no random-design dimension contribution.

The reference follows the stated precision and reliability even when they differ from the generating values. Agreement with it measures how models use the prompt's statistical information; it need not imply optimal prediction under the evaluation distribution. When relevance is uncertain, the posterior probabilities also depend on the prior under irrelevance, which the original prompt leaves unspecified (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

We compute ESS using each model's demonstrations-only curve and report the median over $n\in\{1,2,4\}$. Applied to the reference, this estimator gives $34.6$ at $c=36$, rather than the idealized $36$, because of finite-task sampling and interpolation. We also regress predictions on $m$, $\bar y$, and an intercept. The coefficient on $m$ is the \emph{fitted description weight}; the reference weight is $c/(c+n)$ when relevance is asserted.

\subsection{Reasoning and Stated Precision}\label{sec:once}\label{sec:reasoning}

\paragraph{Descriptions are underweighted without reasoning.}
For $c=36$, summary ESS ranges from $2.0$ to $10.3$ across the five models without reasoning, compared with $34.6$ for the reference (Figure~\ref{fig:llm}a). Fitted weights likewise show substantially less reliance on the description. Sensitivity to stated precision varies: describing the same center as $c=4$ instead of $c=36$ reduces reference ESS from $34.6$ to $13.7$, GPT-6 Sol's from $10.3$ to $2.4$, and Opus~4.6's and Gemma~4 31B's by about half; Qwen3.5 9B and gpt-oss-20b change less. At $n=4$, GPT-6 Sol's fitted weights are $0.47$ and $0.19$ at stated $c=36$ and $4$, and Opus~4.6's are $0.40$ and $0.20$, against reference weights $0.90$ and $0.50$.

\paragraph{Reasoning improves the use of stated precision.}
With reasoning enabled, Opus~4.6 and Gemma~4 31B attain approximately the reference ESS in both conditions, and GPT-6 Sol comes close. Haiku~5.5 at medium effort falls short ($28$ and $8.5$, against $34.6$ and $13.7$), mainly at $n=1$, where $21$--$28\%$ of replies contain no recorded reasoning output. On tasks where $m$ and $\bar y$ are sufficiently separated, $89$--$100\%$ of these four models' replies at $n=4$ imply description weights within $0.05$ of the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

Qwen3.5 9B and gpt-oss-20b also approach the reference weights, despite lower reported HLE scores (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). Their demonstrations-only curves flatten at larger $n$, and gpt-oss-20b makes substantial averaging errors. Some ESS crossings therefore fall beyond the grid. Squares in Figure~\ref{fig:llm} use the reference's demonstrations-only curve and report $t^*_{\mathrm{ref}}-n$, where that curve reaches the model's error at $t^*_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The remaining points use each model's own baseline.

\subsection{Stated Reliability}

A stated $50\%$ chance of irrelevance reduces reference ESS from $34.6$ to $5.7$ under the uniform baseline prior. Without reasoning, most models show a much smaller reduction; GPT-6 Sol falls from $10.3$ to $0.9$. With reasoning, GPT-6 Sol, Gemma~4 31B, and Qwen3.5 9B closely match predictions obtained by \emph{averaging with prior weights}. Each component prediction conditions on the demonstrations, while its weight remains $p$ or $1-p$. In the scalar approximation, the resulting weight on $m$ is $pc/(c+n)$ (Table~\ref{tab:hedge}).

Bayesian model averaging uses posterior component probabilities, which depend on both component marginal likelihoods. The original warning leaves the baseline prior unspecified, saying only that the mean is otherwise “unknown.” We therefore retest GPT-6 Sol at low effort and Haiku~5.5 at medium effort with that prior stated explicitly: the mean “was instead drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 100 to 999.”

With the baseline prior stated and a $50\%$ probability of irrelevance, both models' predictions are closer to the Bayesian reference. On tasks where posterior and prior weighting give predictions that differ by at least $5$, the share of replies closer to the reference rises from $0$--$5\%$ to $92$--$97\%$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $21$--$41\%$ to $87$--$95\%$ for Haiku~5.5.

To test whether description weights vary with the evidence, we divide each reply's description weight by $c/(c+n)$. Under the scalar approximation in Equation~\eqref{eq:scalar-rule}, the reference's normalized weight equals its posterior relevance probability, $\pi_n$. Regressing the models' normalized weights on $\pi_n$ gives slopes $0.96$ and $0.98$ for GPT-6 Sol and Haiku~5.5, respectively, compared with $0.01$ and $0.22$ under the original warning (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}). In separate wording experiments, both models often repeat $m$ under “give or take” and “$\pm$” formulations, which do not define a prior standard deviation.

\subsection{A Semantic Task}

We next consider a message-filing task with a uniform prior over $60$ hidden policies. Policy notes express constraints in ordinary language: a broad note leaves $20$ compatible policies, and a specific note leaves $2$. The model must interpret the note to determine which policies remain possible (Appendix~\ref{app:llm}).

With reasoning, Haiku~5.5 and GPT-6 Sol obtain ESS $9.9$ and $9.5$ for the specific note at $n=2$, compared with $11.4$ for the reference. For unreliable notes, the generator draws the note independently of the task with probability $0.5$, and the prompt specifies this component. We compare Bayesian predictions with those obtained by retaining the prior component weights, except when the demonstrations rule out the note. Among queries where these predictions differ detectably, $77$--$99\%$ of the two models' predictions are closer to the Bayesian reference. The agreement with posterior weighting therefore extends to ordinary-language constraints within this finite task family.

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Bayesian accounts of ICL.}
Theoretical work studies ICL as Bayesian inference over latent tasks \citep{xie2022,panwar2024}, as a learning algorithm implemented in a forward pass \citep{akyurek2023,vonoswald2023}, and as meta-learning across tasks \citep{garg2022,muller2022}. Meta-trained predictors can approximate Bayesian inference under suitable training conditions \citep{genewein2025}. Agreement can change with training-task diversity or new function classes \citep{raventos2023,panwar2024}, and language-model ICL can violate Bayesian consistency \citep{falck2024}.

Additional task information has been studied through input-distribution descriptions \citep{huangge2025}, candidate hypotheses \citep{lin2025}, task-group mixtures \citep{lin2024}, changing instruction influence \citep{tong2026}, and related datasets supplied as context \citep{zhu2026}. We use explicit Bayesian predictors to quantify how such information changes the demonstrations needed for a given prediction error.

\paragraph{Prior effective sample size.}
Prior ESS expresses the influence of a prior in observation units, with different definitions matching different statistical quantities \citep{morita2008,neuenschwander2020}. Our comparison is closest to measures based on expected loss \citep{reimherr2021,wiesenfarth2020}. \citet{lescao2021} measure the average horizontal separation of learning curves in prompt-based fine-tuning. \citet{selby2025} compare language-model-elicited priors with data-based predictors by the number of observations needed to match predictive loss. We adapt performance matching to task descriptions at a specified demonstration count, using each learner's own baseline curve, and analyze how precision, dimension, and relevance probability determine predictive ESS.

\paragraph{Descriptions and evidence in language models.}
Task summaries distilled from demonstrations can replace the examples in context \citep{honda2025}. Other studies report weak sensitivity to instruction relevance \citep{webson2022} or limited benefits from repository instruction files \citep{gloaguen2026}. \citet{gupta2025} compare explicitly stated coin biases with observations and evaluate the predictions against Bayesian references. Concurrent work studies how reasoning and code execution affect language models' approximation of Bayesian posteriors \citep{filbry2026}. Our numerical experiments vary stated precision and reliability separately, with controls that hold the description center and demonstrations fixed. Code execution is disabled in the main runs. Appendix~\ref{app:related} discusses further connections.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

A task description's value depends on its precision, its reliability, and the demonstrations already available. In our Gaussian model, a reliable description can replace many demonstrations by constraining the task across directions. Uncertain relevance imposes a finite ESS ceiling before demonstrations are observed. Demonstrations can then supply evidence about relevance or constrain directions left unspecified by a partial description, increasing its ESS.

Meta-trained Transformers approach the Bayes-optimal benchmark, while their response to reliability shifts reflects training. With reasoning enabled, most tested pretrained language models produce description weights closer to the Bayesian reference. For the two models tested with an explicit irrelevant-component prior, agreement with posterior weighting extends from numerical predictions to semantic policy constraints. Incomplete or informal probabilistic specifications produce different behavior, making the stated model assumptions consequential to the comparison.

The analysis offers a controlled interpretation of why descriptions sometimes substitute for many examples \citep{lescao2021,honda2025} and sometimes have limited influence \citep{webson2022,gupta2025,gloaguen2026}. It identifies distinct possible causes: low specificity, uncertain relevance, and a learner's limited use of the available information. Establishing which cause explains a practical prompting result requires evaluating that setting directly.

\paragraph{Scope and limitations.}
The theory assumes Gaussian linear regression, squared error, and a particular mixture model of irrelevance. Learner-specific ESS measures improvement relative to a learner's own baseline and should be read alongside absolute error. The language-model tasks are small and controlled; the main numerical comparison uses $200$ tasks, and the explicit-prior and semantic experiments cover two models. Some reasoning comparisons also differ in serving, decoding, or readout settings. GPT-6 Sol's results without reasoning depend on its agent configuration. Agreement with a Bayesian reference concerns predictions and does not identify the model's internal computation. Natural descriptions with unstated uncertainty, more structured tasks, and richer or heavy-tailed priors \citep{dalal1983,ohagan2012} would test how far these findings extend.

\section*{Impact Statement}
This work studies how learners combine task descriptions with demonstrations. Its measurement framework may inform prompt evaluation and design when descriptions are uncertain or incomplete. The experiments use synthetic tasks; their results do not establish dependable instruction following in consequential applications. Broader implications depend on how the framework is extended and used.

\subsection{Reliable Descriptions}\label{app:specificity}

\paragraph{Notation and risk.}
Write $\varepsilon = 1/a_0$ for the baseline prior precision. Let $X_n\in\R^{n\times d}$ contain the demonstration inputs as rows, and set $W_n=X_n^\top X_n$. For a prior precision $\kappa>0$, define
\[
R^{\kappa}(n)=\E\operatorname{tr}(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
Thus $R^{\mathrm{demo}}=R^{\varepsilon}$ and $R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}=R^c$. Here and throughout, risk means expected squared prediction error against the noiseless query mean $w^\top x$. The identities follow from the Gaussian posterior covariance: the posterior mean minimizes conditional squared error, and the independent query satisfies $\E[xx^\top]=I_d$. Averaging the conditional error $x^\top\Sigma x$ therefore gives $\E\operatorname{tr}\Sigma$.

Throughout the proofs, $d$ is a positive integer, $a_0>0$, $c\ge\varepsilon$, $r=1/(ca_0)\in(0,1]$, and $p\in[0,1]$. Specificity is represented by the relevant component's precision $c$; $r$ is its variance as a fraction of the baseline variance. Reliability $p$ is the prior probability of relevance. Noninteger demonstration counts refer to linear interpolation between the risks at adjacent integers.

\paragraph{Wishart identities.}
We use three standard properties of $W_n$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(W\arabic*),leftmargin=*]
    \item If $n<d$, then $W_n$ has rank $n$ almost surely. Consequently, $(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}$ has $d-n$ eigenvalues equal to $1/\kappa$.
    \item If $n\ge d$ and $e$ is a fixed unit vector, then $(e^\top W_n^{-1}e)^{-1}\sim\chi^2_{n-d+1}$ \citep[Theorem~3.2.12]{muirhead1982}. Hence
    \[
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-1}=\frac{d}{n-d-1}\quad(n\ge d+2),
    \]
    whereas this expectation is infinite for $n=d$ and $n=d+1$.
    \item For fixed $d$, as $n\to\infty$,
    \[
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-2}=\frac{d}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}),
    \qquad
    \E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-3}=O(n^{-3})
    \]
    \citep{vonrosen1988}.
\end{enumerate}
The Sherman--Morrison identity implies that an additional demonstration strictly decreases the expected trace of the posterior covariance. Thus $R^\kappa(n)$ is strictly decreasing, and (W2) implies that it tends to zero. Its linear interpolation is therefore continuous and strictly decreasing. In the correctly specified joint model, conditioning on the description cannot increase expected posterior variance, so $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)\le R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)$ and the Bayes-optimal ESS is nonnegative.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:reliable}]
A reliable description without demonstrations has risk
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(0)=\frac{d}{c}=ra_0d.
\]

\emph{(i) Fixed variance reduction.}
By (W1), $R^\varepsilon(n)\ge(d-n)a_0$ for $n<d$; the inequality also holds trivially for $n\ge d$. Linear interpolation preserves this bound, so
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)\ge(d-t)a_0\qquad(t\ge0).
\]
For $t<(1-r)d$, the right-hand side exceeds $ra_0d$. The matching sample size must therefore satisfy $\ess_0\ge(1-r)d$.

For every integer $n$,
\[
\frac{R^\varepsilon(n)}{a_0}
=\E\operatorname{tr}(I_d+a_0W_n)^{-1}
\longrightarrow(d-n)^+
\qquad\text{as }a_0\to\infty.
\]
Indeed, an eigenvalue $1/(1+a_0\lambda)$ is bounded by one and converges to the indicator of $\lambda=0$, so dominated convergence and the almost-sure rank of $W_n$ apply. The same limit holds at every real $t$ by interpolation. For fixed $r\in(0,1)$, the limiting curve crosses $rd$ at the unique point $t=(1-r)d$. Strict monotonicity then gives convergence of the crossing. For $r=1$, the description is uninformative and $\ess_0=0$ for every $a_0$, giving the same conclusion.

\emph{(ii) Fixed precision.}
For fixed $c>0$, the description risk $d/c$ is independent of $a_0$. As $a_0\to\infty$, monotone convergence, (W1), and (W2) give
\[
R^\varepsilon(n)\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
+\infty,&n\le d+1,\\[2pt]
\displaystyle\frac{d}{n-d-1},&n\ge d+2.
\end{cases}
\]
If $c$ is a positive integer, the finite part of this limiting curve equals $d/c$ at the integer $n=c+d+1$. For every fixed point below this crossing the limiting risk is larger than $d/c$, and for every fixed point above it the limiting risk is smaller. The crossing of the interpolated finite-$a_0$ risk therefore converges to $c+d+1$. The integer condition ensures that the equality occurs at a grid point; linear interpolation does not in general preserve the same expression for noninteger $c$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Large-sample expansion.}
For fixed $d$ and $\kappa$, commuting $W_n$ with $\kappa I_d$ gives
\[
(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}
=W_n^{-1}-\kappa W_n^{-2}
+\kappa^2W_n^{-2}(\kappa I_d+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
The final term is positive semidefinite and bounded above by $\kappa^2W_n^{-3}$. Using (W2) and (W3),
\begin{equation}\label{eq:extra}
R^\kappa(n)=\frac{d}{n-d-1}-\frac{\kappa d}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\end{equation}
An additional demonstration reduces the leading term by $d/n^2+O(n^{-3})$. A fixed increase in prior precision and a fixed increase in the demonstration count therefore reduce risk at the same asymptotic order. This comparison gives the large-sample ESS limits below.

\subsection{Unreliable Descriptions}\label{app:reliability}

Let $\mathcal P_n=(D_n,m,x)$ denote the full prompt, including the query input. The posterior relevance probability is $\pi_n=P(Z=1\mid\mathcal P_n)$, whereas $p$ remains the prior relevance probability. Conditional on $Z=1$, the posterior parameters are
\[
\Sigma_1=(cI_d+W_n)^{-1},
\qquad
\mu_1=\Sigma_1(c m+X_n^\top y_{1:n}).
\]
Conditional on $Z=0$, the description center $m$ is independent of $w$, and
\[
\Sigma_0=(\varepsilon I_d+W_n)^{-1},
\qquad
\mu_0=\Sigma_0X_n^\top y_{1:n}.
\]
The posterior relevance probability follows from the two Gaussian marginal likelihoods,
\begin{align*}
L_1&=\mathcal N\!\left(y_{1:n};X_nm,I_n+c^{-1}X_nX_n^\top\right),\\
L_0&=\mathcal N\!\left(y_{1:n};0,I_n+a_0X_nX_n^\top\right),\\
\pi_n&=\frac{pL_1}{pL_1+(1-p)L_0}.
\end{align*}
The query input does not enter this update because it is independent of $(Z,m,D_n)$. Without demonstrations, both marginal likelihoods equal one and $\pi_0=p$, since $m$ has the same distribution under both relevance states. At $p=0$ or $p=1$, the posterior contains only the corresponding component. At $r=1$, $m=0$ almost surely and the two components coincide, so the description provides no additional task information for any $p$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:unreliable}]
As in Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999}, the posterior mean prediction averages the component predictions using their posterior probabilities:
\[
\E[w^\top x\mid\mathcal P_n]
=\big(\pi_n\mu_1+(1-\pi_n)\mu_0\big)^\top x.
\]
The law of total variance over $Z$ separates posterior variance into within-component and between-component terms:
\begin{align*}
\operatorname{Var}(w^\top x\mid\mathcal P_n)
={}&\pi_n x^\top\Sigma_1x+(1-\pi_n)x^\top\Sigma_0x\\
&+\pi_n(1-\pi_n)\big((\mu_1-\mu_0)^\top x\big)^2.
\end{align*}
For $0<p<1$ and any integrable function $g$ of the prompt,
\[
\E[\pi_ng(\mathcal P_n)]=\E[Zg(\mathcal P_n)]
=p\,\E[g(\mathcal P_n)\mid Z=1].
\]
Conditional on $Z=1$, the prompt follows the reliable-description model. Conditional on $Z=0$, the description provides no information about the task. Taking expectations in the variance decomposition therefore gives
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)
=pR^{\mathrm{desc}}_{p=1}(n)+(1-p)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)+\mathcal V_n,
\]
with $\mathcal V_n$ as in Equation~\eqref{eq:decomp}. The endpoint cases $p=0$ and $p=1$ follow directly from the single-component posteriors.

At $n=0$, $\mu_1=m$, $\mu_0=0$, and the prediction is $p\,m^\top x$. For each coordinate,
\[
\operatorname{Var}(w_i)=a_0,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(m_i)=(1-r)a_0,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(w_i,m_i)=p(1-r)a_0.
\]
It follows that
\[
\E(w_i-pm_i)^2
=a_0-2p^2(1-r)a_0+p^2(1-r)a_0
=\big(1-p^2(1-r)\big)a_0.
\]
Since $\E[xx^\top]=I_d$, the prediction risk is $\E\|w-pm\|^2$. Summing over coordinates proves Equation~\eqref{eq:zero}.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}]
\emph{(i) No demonstrations.}
For $0<p<1$ and finite $c$,
\[
R^{\mathrm{desc}}(0)
=\big(1-p^2(1-r)\big)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0)
>(1-p^2)R^{\mathrm{demo}}(0).
\]
The risk on the left decreases to the right-hand side as $c\to\infty$, equivalently $r\to0$. The interpolated baseline risk is continuous, strictly decreasing, and tends to zero. Consequently, the matching sample size is strictly below the unique finite $n_p^{\sharp}$ defined in the corollary and converges to it as $c\to\infty$. At $p=0$, the description is always irrelevant, so $\ess_0=n_0^{\sharp}=0$; the non-strict bound remains valid.

The baseline lower bound and normalized limit established in Appendix~\ref{app:specificity} also give
\[
\ess_0\ge p^2(1-r)d,
\qquad
\ess_0\longrightarrow p^2(1-r)d
\]
when $a_0\to\infty$ with $r$ fixed. Applying the same argument to the level $1-p^2$ gives $n_p^{\sharp}\to p^2d$. These expressions are limits as the baseline prior becomes diffuse; they need not hold exactly at finite $a_0$.

For comparison, an oracle predictor that observes $Z$ has zero-demonstration risk
\[
\big(1-p(1-r)\big)a_0d\ge(1-p)a_0d.
\]
The oracle's zero-demonstration ESS ceiling therefore approaches $pd$ as $a_0\to\infty$. For the learner that must infer relevance, this limit is $p^2d$. The difference is the cost of uncertainty about relevance.

\emph{(ii) Many demonstrations.}
Fix $d$, $a_0$, and $c$, and define the oracle risk
\[
L(n)=pR^c(n)+(1-p)R^\varepsilon(n).
\]
Equation~\eqref{eq:extra} implies, uniformly over integers $0\le t\le T=\lceil c\rceil+1$,
\[
R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n+t)-L(n)
=\frac{d}{n^2}\left[p(c-\varepsilon)-t+O(n^{-1})\right].
\]
Linear interpolation gives the same expansion for real $t\in[0,T]$. Let $n+\eta_n$ be the sample size at which the baseline risk equals $L(n)$. Then
\[
\eta_n=p(c-\varepsilon)+O(n^{-1}).
\]
When $p(c-\varepsilon)=0$, the two risks coincide and $\eta_n=0$ exactly. If $p=1$, the description risk equals $L(n)$, so
\[
\ess_n\longrightarrow c-\varepsilon=(1-r)c.
\]
If $p<1$, the variance decomposition gives $R^{\mathrm{desc}}(n)=L(n)+\mathcal V_n\ge L(n)$. Since the baseline risk decreases with sample size, $\ess_n\le\eta_n$, proving
\[
\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ess_n\le p(c-\varepsilon)=p(1-r)c.
\]
Because $\mathcal V_n$ is nonnegative and need not vanish at the required rate, the result for unreliable descriptions is an upper bound.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Partial Descriptions}\label{app:partial}

A reliable description may constrain only $k<d$ directions. Suppose the description leaves a fraction $r$ of the baseline variance in these directions and leaves the remaining $d-k$ directions unchanged. The description-conditioned prior precision is $c$ in the constrained directions and $\varepsilon=1/a_0$ elsewhere. Write
\[
\Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(cI_k,\varepsilon I_{d-k}).
\]
The prediction risk is $\E\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}$. Rotational invariance of $W_n$ means that the result depends on the number of constrained directions, not on their orientation.

\begin{proposition}[ESS of a partial description]\label{prop:partial}
Let $p=1$ and let $0\le k<d$ be an integer.
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item[(i)] As $a_0\to\infty$, $\ess_0\to(1-r)k$ with $r$ fixed, and $\ess_0\to k$ with $c$ fixed.
    \item[(ii)] For fixed $d$, $a_0$, and $c$, as $n\to\infty$,
    \[
    \ess_n\longrightarrow\frac{k}{d}(c-1/a_0)
    =\frac{k}{d}(1-r)c.
    \]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

As the baseline prior becomes diffuse with $c$ fixed, the unconstrained directions dominate the zero-demonstration risk, and ESS approaches the number of constrained directions, $k$. After many demonstrations, ESS instead approaches the added precision averaged across all $d$ directions. A sufficiently precise partial description can therefore be worth more alongside demonstrations than by itself. Table~\ref{tab:partial} illustrates this complementarity: at $c=100$, a description of half of the $d=16$ directions has ESS $8.1$ without demonstrations and $36$ with $100$ demonstrations.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}]
\emph{(i) No demonstrations.}
The description risk is
\[
\operatorname{tr}\Lambda^{-1}=(kr+d-k)a_0.
\]
Using $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(t)\ge(d-t)a_0$ gives $\ess_0\ge(1-r)k$. The normalized baseline risk converges to $(d-t)^+$, locally uniformly in $t$, by the integer limits and linear interpolation. With $r$ fixed, the normalized description risk is $d-(1-r)k$. With $c$ fixed, it is
\[
d-k+\frac{k}{ca_0}\longrightarrow d-k.
\]
Because $k<d$, both limiting levels are positive and have unique crossings, at $(1-r)k$ and $k$, respectively. Convergence of the crossings proves the claim. When the description leaves the prior unchanged, its ESS is identically zero.

\emph{(ii) Many demonstrations.}
Applying the resolvent identity twice gives
\[
(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}
=W_n^{-1}-W_n^{-1}\Lambda W_n^{-1}
+W_n^{-1}\Lambda W_n^{-1}\Lambda(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}.
\]
Rotational invariance implies
\[
\E W_n^{-2}=\frac{\E\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-2}}{d}I_d.
\]
The expected trace of the middle term is therefore
$\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda)/n^2+O(n^{-3})$ by (W3). To bound the remainder, use $|\operatorname{tr}(MB)|\le\|M\|_{\mathrm{op}}\operatorname{tr}B$ for $B\succeq0$, together with $(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}\preceq W_n^{-1}$. Its absolute trace is at most
\[
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\|W_n^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-1}
\le d\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{op}}^2\operatorname{tr}W_n^{-3},
\]
whose expectation is $O(n^{-3})$. Consequently,
\[
\E\operatorname{tr}(\Lambda+W_n)^{-1}
=\frac{d}{n-d-1}-\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Lambda}{n^2}+O(n^{-3}).
\]
Matching this expansion to the baseline risk, as in Corollary~\ref{cor:ess}(ii), gives
\[
\ess_n\longrightarrow\frac{\operatorname{tr}\Lambda-d\varepsilon}{d}
=\frac{k(c-\varepsilon)}{d}.
\]
\end{proof}
The same expansion applies to any fixed Gaussian description precision matrix $\Lambda\succeq\varepsilon I_d$ compatible with the baseline task distribution. Its large-sample ESS is the added precision averaged across directions, $(\operatorname{tr}\Lambda-d\varepsilon)/d$.

\subsection{Assuming Relevance Under Misspecification}\label{app:misspecification}

Consider the Bayesian predictor that sets $p=1$ in its inference model and therefore returns $\mu_1^\top x$. We evaluate its risk, denoted $R^{\mathrm{assume}}(n)$, under the data-generating model with relevance probability $p$. The assumed prior is misspecified when $p<1$ and $r<1$, because it omits the irrelevant component.

Without demonstrations, this predictor returns $m^\top x$. The moments used in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:unreliable} give
\[
R^{\mathrm{assume}}(0)
=\E\|w-m\|^2
=\big(1-(2p-1)(1-r)\big)a_0d.
\]
For $r<1$, this risk exceeds the baseline $a_0d$ exactly when $p<1/2$. At $r=1$, the description carries no information, $m=0$ almost surely, and the two risks coincide for every $p$.

With demonstrations, the predictor continues to use $\mu_1^\top x$ regardless of $Z$, where
\[
\mu_1=m+(cI_d+W_n)^{-1}X_n^\top(y_{1:n}-X_nm).
\]
For fixed $a_0$ and $n$, $\mu_1\to m$ in mean square as $c\to\infty$. Conditional on relevance, $\E\|w-m\|^2=ra_0d\to0$; conditional on irrelevance, it is $(2-r)a_0d\to2a_0d$. Thus
\[
\lim_{c\to\infty}R^{\mathrm{assume}}(n)=2(1-p)a_0d.
\]
For every $p<1$, this is a positive risk floor at fixed $n$, whereas $R^{\mathrm{demo}}(n)\to0$ as $n\to\infty$. Hence a sufficiently precise description can increase risk relative to the demonstrations-only predictor when relevance is assumed despite $p<1$. At $p=0.9$, $c=100$, $d=16$, and $a_0=10$, the predictor assuming relevance has risk $25.9$, compared with $26.6$ without the description, at $n=14$. At $n=15$, these risks are $25.5$ and $19.5$, respectively. Its risk remains higher at every larger demonstration count evaluated, up to $10^5$. The order of limits matters: the preceding argument takes $c\to\infty$ at fixed $n$; it does not imply that demonstrations cannot overcome a fixed finite prior precision.

\paragraph{Architecture and optimization.}
The Transformer has $12$ layers, $8$ attention heads, and width $256$, matching the model size of \citet{garg2022}. We train with Adam at learning rate $10^{-4}$ for $40{,}000$ steps, using batches of $1{,}024$ newly sampled prompts and no curriculum. Following \citet{zhu2026}, the model predicts a mean and a log variance for the query label and minimizes log loss. The optimal predicted mean is the posterior mean, which also minimizes squared error. We evaluate the predicted mean using the loss in Equation~\eqref{eq:regret}.

Each demonstration token contains an input and its label. Two indicator coordinates distinguish token types, and the model uses no positional encoding. When a description is absent, its token is masked out of attention. The prediction is read from the query token. Labels and description centers are divided by $\sqrt{a_0}$, gradients are clipped to norm $1$, and training uses bf16 autocast. The demonstration count is sampled uniformly from $0$ to $20$, except for the $c = 0.2$ and $c = 2$ experiments, which use $0$ to $15$, and the $d = 10$ experiments, which use $0$ to $40$.

Layers use pre-normalization, GELU activations, feed-forward width $1{,}024$, and no dropout. At $d = 5$, the model has $9.5$ million parameters. Adam uses the PyTorch defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, and $\epsilon = 10^{-8}$, with no weight decay and a constant learning rate. Training seeds are $0$, $1$, and $2$ for $c = 10$, for $d = 10$, and for the partial-description experiments; $0$ and $1$ for $c = 1$; and $0$ for $c \in \{0.2,2\}$. A $d = 5$ network takes approximately $80$ minutes to train on one NVIDIA L4 GPU, and a $d = 10$ network takes approximately $2.8$ hours. Training all $25$ networks takes approximately $38$ GPU-hours.

Without a description, every $d = 5$ network's error is within $0.21$ of the Bayes-optimal error of approximately $50$ at $n = 0$, and within $0.32$ on average over its training demonstration counts. At $d = 10$, the corresponding gaps are at most $0.50$ relative to an error of approximately $100$, and $0.41$. With a description at $c = 10$, $p = 1$, and $n = 0$, network error ranges from $0.67$ to $0.71$, compared with $0.49$ for the Bayes-optimal predictor. The networks therefore remove $98.6\%$ of the baseline error, compared with $99.0\%$ for the Bayes-optimal predictor.

\paragraph{Additional specificity levels.}
Four additional networks cover $c = 0.2$ and $c = 2$, each at $p = 1$ and $p = 0.9$. Their zero-demonstration ESS values are within $0.03$ of the Bayes-optimal values evaluated on the same prompts: $2.64$ versus $2.66$, $2.16$ versus $2.15$, $7.32$ versus $7.35$, and $4.38$ versus $4.37$, respectively.

\paragraph{Higher dimension and partial descriptions.}
At $d = 10$, we train three seeds with description precision $c = 10$ and extend training and evaluation to $40$ demonstrations so that the demonstrations-only learning curve reaches the ESS crossing. Network ESS is $17.8$, compared with a Bayes-optimal value of $20.8$ and the limiting expression $c + d + 1 = 21$. As at $d = 5$, the networks capture most of the description's predictive value but fall short of the Bayes-optimal ESS.

We also train networks with a partial description, as defined in Appendix~\ref{app:partial}. The description token contains $m$ in the first $k = 3$ of the $d = 5$ coordinates and zeros elsewhere; the remaining task coordinates follow the baseline prior. For this $c = 10$ description, mean ESS across three seeds increases from $3.27$ at $n = 0$ to $5.55$ at $n = 10$. The corresponding Bayes-optimal values are $3.22$ and $5.64$. This increase agrees with the behavior described by Proposition~\ref{prop:partial}.

\paragraph{ESS with demonstrations.}
Figure~\ref{fig:networks-ess-n} evaluates each network at its training reliability. For $c = 1$, network ESS remains within $0.6$ of the Bayes-optimal value at every demonstration count whose crossing lies within the evaluation grid. For $c = 10$, the largest shortfalls are $2.4$, $2.9$, and $1.4$ demonstrations at $p = 1$, $0.99$, and $0.9$, respectively; the largest excess is $0.3$. At $n = 5$, the three $c = 10$ networks trained at $p = 0.9$ have ESS values of $4.83$, $4.79$, and $4.77$, compared with a Bayes-optimal value of $5.20$ on the same prompts.

\paragraph{Reliability shift after demonstrations are observed.}
For $c = 10$, networks trained at $p = 1$ and $p = 0.99$ both have fitted description weights near one at $n = 0$. With demonstrations, their responses to a shift in test reliability differ. At test reliability $p = 0.9$, the description reduces error for networks trained at $p = 0.99$ at every evaluated $n$, as it does for the Bayes-optimal predictor under the test distribution. For networks trained at $p = 1$, the description instead increases error from six demonstrations onward in every seed. The excess error ranges from $0.77$ to $1.06$ at $n = 6$ and peaks between $2.74$ and $2.94$ at $n = 9$. A Bayesian predictor that assumes relevance ($p = 1$) also first has higher error than the demonstrations-only predictor at $n = 6$, with excess error $1.12$ there and $2.96$ at $n = 9$.

\section{Additional Language-Model Results and Evaluation Details}\label{app:llm}

We generate $2{,}000$ tasks. Six open-weight models answer all tasks without reasoning; we refer to these experiments as the \emph{full run}. The models are gpt-oss-20b, Qwen3.5 9B, and Gemma~4 31B, which also appear in Section~\ref{sec:llm}, together with Phi-4 (14B; \citealp{abdin2024}), OLMo~2 32B Instruct \citep{walsh2025}, and Gemma~4 12B. The main numerical experiments in Section~\ref{sec:llm} use the first $200$ of these tasks. The semantic experiment uses a separate set of $200$ tasks, described in Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}. Unless stated otherwise, the results in this appendix use the full run without reasoning.

Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports summary ESS, defined as the median over $n \in \{1,2,4\}$, with uncertainty intervals. Figure~\ref{fig:curves} shows the underlying learning curves. Tables~\ref{tab:llm-worth} and~\ref{tab:llm-worth-written} report ESS at every evaluated demonstration count; Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} compares interpolation methods; Table~\ref{tab:llm-full} reports description weights for the models with a distribution readout; and Table~\ref{tab:mixture} evaluates mixtures of relevant and irrelevant descriptions.

For the numerical experiments, the \emph{prompt-based Bayesian reference} combines the probabilistic statements in the prompt with an assumed baseline prior when the prompt does not specify one. It is optimal under that assumed model, but need not minimize error under the actual task distribution when the two differ. We therefore distinguish this reference from the correctly specified Bayesian predictor in the theory. The semantic experiment specifies a finite task distribution and uses its exact Bayesian predictor.

In the numerical task, $c=\sigma^2/\tau^2$ is the prior precision expressed in units of one demonstration. We distinguish the precision used to generate a description value from the precision stated in the prompt whenever they differ. Similarly, a stated reliability supplies a prior relevance probability to the model; it need not equal the proportion of relevant descriptions in the evaluation sample.

\subsection{Prompts, Tasks, and Model Readouts}

\paragraph{Model inclusion.}
We retain a model only if it produces a number in at least three quarters of replies overall and, averaged over $n=1$, $2$, and $4$ and both noise levels, in at least half of replies without a description. The latter responses determine the baseline curve used for summary ESS. This criterion excludes MiniCPM5~2B \citep{minicpm5}, OLMo~2 7B Instruct, and OLMo~2 13B Instruct, whose replies begin with a number in $62\%$, $20\%$, and $10\%$ of cases, respectively. These models often begin with explanations such as ``To predict\ldots'' or ``Given\ldots'' despite the numeric-only instruction.

The full run uses Hugging Face Transformers at each model's native precision, with checkpoints \path{openai/gpt-oss-20b}, \path{microsoft/phi-4}, \path{allenai/OLMo-2-0325-32B-Instruct}, \path{Qwen/Qwen3.5-9B}, \path{google/gemma-4-12B-it}, and \path{google/gemma-4-31B-it}. Tasks are sampled by a seeded generator. Reasoning is disabled where the chat template supports this option. For gpt-oss-20b, generation begins in the final channel, after the channel normally used for reasoning.

\paragraph{Distribution and written readouts.}
For gpt-oss-20b, Phi-4, and OLMo~2 32B without reasoning, the prediction is the mean of the first-token distribution, renormalized over the $900$ three-digit number tokens. We call this the \emph{distribution readout}. Other models, and all reasoning runs, use the number in the final answer, called the \emph{written readout}. We also evaluate written readouts for the three distribution-readout models as a robustness check (Table~\ref{tab:llm} for gpt-oss-20b, and Table~\ref{tab:paraphrase}). OLMo~2 32B assigns $41\%$ of first-token probability to numbers without a description at $n = 1$, and $75\%$ at $n = 2$. Its distribution readout at $n = 1$ therefore summarizes a minority of the first-token probability mass.

\paragraph{Prompt.}
The following text is supplied as the user turn in the model's chat template:
\begin{quote}\scriptsize\ttfamily\raggedright
The numbers below are drawn from a normal distribution with standard deviation 60.\\
Its mean was drawn from a normal distribution with mean 510 and standard deviation 30.\\[3pt]
548\\
431\\[3pt]
Predict the next number. Reply with the number only.
\end{quote}

Without a description, the second sentence is ``Its mean is unknown.'' To state unreliability, a third sentence is added: ``There is a 10\% chance that this statement is wrong, in which case the mean is unknown.'' In the rerun that states the baseline prior, the final clause becomes ``in which case the mean was instead drawn uniformly at random from the whole numbers 100 to 999.'' With no demonstrations, the final question is ``Predict the first number.''

\paragraph{Task generation.}
A relevant description value is sampled conditional on the task so that the joint distribution satisfies $m \sim \mathcal{N}(500,100^2-\tau^2)$ and $w\mid m \sim \mathcal{N}(m,\tau^2)$. A task is redrawn if a displayed number or description value falls outside $100$ to $999$; this occurs for less than $1\%$ of tasks. The full run also includes $\sigma = 30$ conditions. Both standard deviations stated in the prompt are then halved, preserving $c \in \{1,4,36\}$. The qualitative pattern is unchanged (Table~\ref{tab:llm-worth}).

\paragraph{Numeric response rates.}
For the distribution readout, the numeric response rate is the probability mass assigned to number tokens at the first position. For the written readout, it is the fraction of replies containing a three-digit number. Tables~\ref{tab:paraphrase}, \ref{tab:llm-worth}, \ref{tab:llm-worth-written}, and~\ref{tab:llm-full} report this quantity as ``number''. Tables~\ref{tab:paraphrase} and~\ref{tab:llm-full} italicize cells whose rate is below three quarters.

\paragraph{Written-readout implementation and checks.}
The written readout extracts the number in the last \verb|\boxed{}| expression in the final answer, if present. Otherwise, it takes the last number with three digits before an optional decimal part, retaining the decimals. A digit group after a thousands separator, as in $14{,}945$, does not count as a qualifying number. In the full run, decoding is greedy with a limit of eight new tokens, so the reply is the number itself. Those predictions were recorded using an earlier readout that took the first qualifying number and retained only its integer part. Replies without a qualifying number are excluded from both error curves and weight regressions; the reported response rates identify the extent of this exclusion.

For gpt-oss-20b, written-readout weights differ from distribution-readout weights by at most $0.21$ across all conditions and demonstration counts. For $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$, written-readout summary ESS is $0.2$, $2.0$, and $3.7$, compared with $0.9$, $2.0$, and $2.8$ from the distribution readout. A single greedy response is a noisier summary than the distribution mean. Phi-4 produces a number in only $58\%$ of written replies across all conditions, so Table~\ref{tab:llm} reports only its distribution readout. In the wording experiment, however, its numeric response rate exceeds three quarters for the original wording. OLMo~2 32B produces a number in only $5\%$, $36\%$, and $48\%$ of written replies without a description at $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$, respectively, with $\sigma = 60$; we therefore do not report its written-readout ESS. Its written-readout weights for the three relevant descriptions at $n = 1$ are $0.83$, $0.81$, and $0.83$, compared with distribution-readout weights of $0.72$, $0.70$, and $0.70$.

\subsection{ESS Estimation and the Bayesian Reference}

\paragraph{Measurement.}
We apply Definition~\ref{def:ess} to squared error against the hidden mean. In the full run, the baseline curve is evaluated at $n = 1$, $2$, $4$, $\ldots$, $64$. We interpolate squared error, rather than its square root, linearly in $\log n$. The ESS at $n$ is the first interpolated demonstration count $t$ that matches the description-conditioned error, minus $n$.

If the description-conditioned error lies below the entire baseline curve up to $t_{\max}$, we report $\ess_n > t_{\max}-n$. Because the curve starts at one demonstration, we use the marker $\ess_n \leq 1-n$ when one demonstration alone already gives lower error. These markers record censoring at the evaluation boundaries; they do not define an extrapolated matching count or an exact ESS when the crossing does not exist. Summary ESS uses $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$. We exclude $n = 0$ because models rarely return a number without a description or demonstrations. At large $n$, ESS is strongly affected by slow baseline learning and excessive weight on the $c = 1$ and $c = 4$ descriptions. At $\sigma = 60$ and $n = 64$, model root-mean-square error without a description ranges from $9$ to $20$, compared with $7.5$ for the reference. A learner with a flatter baseline curve can assign a larger ESS to the same error reduction. Description-weight estimates complement ESS because they do not use that curve.

Bootstrap replicates resample tasks jointly for the description-conditioned and baseline curves and recompute the complete ESS statistic. The intervals therefore measure variation across tasks; they do not include interpolation bias, quantified below. We use $500$ replicates with a fixed seed and the same resampled tasks for every model.

\paragraph{Reference prior and exact sample equivalence.}
The prompt-based Bayesian reference takes ``unknown'' to mean uniform over $100$ to $999$. Without a description, its prediction is therefore approximately the demonstration mean, with negligible boundary truncation for the evaluated prompts. For a relevant Gaussian description with correctly stated precision, the idealized continuous model with a flat baseline prior has exact sample equivalence $c$. Integer rounding, finite-range truncation, and the task-rejection rule are not part of that exact identity; we compute the reported reference values on the same generated tasks as the models. When a chance of being wrong is stated, the reference uses it as the prior probability of irrelevance and computes the posterior relevance probability from the demonstrations. Otherwise, it treats the description as relevant.

Applying the empirical ESS procedure to the full-run reference gives $1.3$, $4.4$, and $36$ for $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$ at $\sigma = 60$, and $1.2$, $4.7$, and $43$ at $\sigma = 30$. Interpolation in $\log n$ induces an upward bias, while finite-task sampling can shift the estimate in either direction. We use the same estimator for the reference and the models. On the $200$ tasks of Section~\ref{sec:llm}, the grid additionally includes $n = 40$ and $48$ near the $c = 36$ crossing, and the reference values are $1.0$, $3.5$, and $34.6$. With a stated chance of irrelevance, the reference's summary ESS varies more across task samples: on $20{,}000$ tasks it is $7.3$ at a stated $50\%$ and $23.4$ at $10\%$, compared with $5.8$ and $22$ on the full run's $2{,}000$ tasks and $5.7$ and $27.5$ on the first $200$. Each model is compared with the reference on the same tasks.

The actual task prior, $\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$, is not stated in the prompt. A reference using that prior would have baseline precision equivalent to $0.36$ demonstrations at $\sigma = 60$ and $0.09$ at $\sigma = 30$. At $\sigma = 60$, the corresponding idealized continuous Gaussian model would give a relevant description exact ESS $c-0.36$, namely $0.64$, $3.64$, and $35.64$. This is the scalar analogue of $c-a_0^{-1}$ in Section~\ref{sec:specificity}. We use the prompt-based reference because the models are not given the actual task prior. This distinction matters under misspecification: unlike the correctly specified Bayesian predictor in Sections~\ref{sec:specificity}--\ref{sec:reliability}, a reference that incorrectly assumes an irrelevant description to be relevant can have negative ESS, indicating increased prediction error \citep{reimherr2021}.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to interpolation.}
The full-run grid doubles $n$, so interpolation can affect ESS. On the reference's idealized errors, $\sigma^2/n$ without a description and $\sigma^2/(c+n)$ with one, interpolation in $\log n$ overestimates ESS by $0.2$, $0.3$, and $1.8$ for $c = 1$, $4$, and $36$. Linear interpolation in $n$ gives overestimates of $0.3$, $0.6$, and $4.1$.

For the models without reasoning, the relevant crossings are unique. Among the twelve baseline curves across the two noise levels, only Phi-4 at $\sigma = 60$ increases, slightly, from $20.3$ at $n = 32$ to $20.4$ at $n = 64$. Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity} compares four estimators: interpolation in $\log n$; linear interpolation in $n$; a least-squares non-increasing fit using the pool-adjacent-violators algorithm; and a relative-least-squares fit of $A/(n+n_0)+B$ to the baseline errors. The final form is a Bayesian learning curve plus an error floor and recovers exact ESS when fit to the reference's idealized errors.

Across these choices, no model's summary ESS changes by more than $1.4$ demonstrations. For the $c = 36$ description, no model exceeds $8.1$, while the reference ranges from $35$ to $38$. Differences become larger at higher demonstration counts, where a flat learning curve turns a small error difference into a large change in ESS. For example, gpt-oss-20b at $n = 8$ and $c = 36$ has ESS $17$ under the estimator in Table~\ref{tab:llm-worth}, but $9.2$ under the fitted curve.

\subsection{Unreliability, Generating Precision, and Wording}

\paragraph{Irrelevant descriptions.}
An irrelevant description uses an independently drawn value. At $c = 36$, these descriptions have negative ESS for every model and for the prompt-based reference, which treats them as relevant. The models assign less weight to the description and consequently incur lower error in this condition: at $n = 4$, their root-mean-square errors range from $35$ to $64$, compared with $124$ for the reference.

\paragraph{Actual unreliability.}
The stated-unreliability experiments keep descriptions relevant and vary what models are told. Table~\ref{tab:mixture} instead evaluates a mixture of relevant and irrelevant descriptions, following the construction in Section~\ref{sec:setting}. We combine squared errors from the relevant and irrelevant conditions, on the same tasks, with weights $p$ and $1-p$, and then estimate ESS. We evaluate models both with and without disclosure of the irrelevance probability. We compare a reference that sets $p=1$ with one that uses the actual reliability and updates its posterior relevance probability from the demonstrations. Both retain the numerical reference's uniform baseline prior, rather than the Gaussian prior of the task generator. Thus, matching the reliability does not make the reference correctly specified for the task generator.

At actual reliability $p = 0.9$, a $c = 36$ description has ESS $5.5$ for the reference that uses the actual reliability and $0.2$ for the reference assuming relevance. Model ESS ranges from $0.4$ to $3.2$ when the reliability is not stated and from $-0.1$ to $4.0$ when it is stated. Model weights depend weakly on $p$, except for Gemma~4 12B at $n=1$, and stating the probability changes ESS by at most two demonstrations in either direction. At $p = 0.5$, the description is worth less than one demonstration to the reference that uses the actual reliability and at most one to every model.

\subsection{Reasoning Runs and Execution Settings}

\paragraph{Comparisons between modes.}
The labels ``with reasoning'' and ``without reasoning'' refer to generation settings. Statements about individual replies refer to recorded reasoning output. Paired runs use the same model identifier and prompts, but reasoning settings, serving software, readout, retry policy, and access to calculation tools vary across the runs. These comparisons concern the evaluated configurations and do not identify a unique internal mechanism.

\paragraph{Reasoning and benchmark performance.}
A higher score on Humanity's Last Exam (HLE) does not imply closer agreement with the reference description weights in these configurations. With reasoning, Qwen3.5 9B and gpt-oss-20b have reported HLE scores of $14.9\%$ and $5.3\%$, respectively, below GPT-6 Sol's $18.4\%$ and Opus~4.6's $19.1\%$ without reasoning in the modes we evaluate \citep{phan2025,aa2026}. \subsection{Prediction Rules and Relevance Inference}

\paragraph{Per-response description weights.}
At $n=4$, we compute the per-response description weight $(\hat y-\bar y)/(m-\bar y)$. This quantity describes one response and differs from the fitted coefficient obtained by regressing predictions across tasks. We use the $61$ tasks per cell with $|m-\bar y|\geq30$ and exclude replies without a number. A reply is classified as consistent with a rule when its per-response weight is within $0.05$ of that rule's weight. The candidate weights are the Bayesian reference ($0.90$ for $c=36$ and $0.20$ for $c=1$); the reference with its two precisions exchanged ($0.10$ and $0.80$); the description counted as one additional observation, $1/(n+1)$; the description alone, with weight $1$; and the demonstrations alone, with weight $0$. The one-additional-observation rule coincides with the reference at $c=1$.

With reasoning, Opus~4.6, GPT-6 Sol, Gemma~4 31B, and Haiku~5.5 match the reference rule on $89$ to $100\%$ of eligible replies. Without reasoning, $77\%$ of Opus~4.6 replies at $c=36$ match none of these candidate rules, as do more than half of GPT-6 Sol replies. Olmo~3 7B Think averages demonstrations well but reaches summary ESS only $6.2$ at $c=36$. At $n=1$, it repeats the description value in $57$ to $80\%$ of replies, more often at higher stated precision. At $n=4$, about half of its eligible replies return the description value alone and a third match the reference. Without a description, its root-mean-square error falls from $56$ at $n=1$ to $10.8$ at $n=32$, compared with $10.0$ for the reference. Its shortfall therefore lies in combining the description with demonstrations. Enabling reasoning does not ensure reference behavior in every model.

\paragraph{Averaging with prior weights.}
For $c=36$, we compare the Bayesian reference with a predictor that conditions each component on the demonstrations and averages the resulting posterior means with prior weights $p$ and $1-p$. In the scalar approximation, this gives weight $p\,36/(36+n)$ to $m$. Table~\ref{tab:hedge} reports fitted description weights under the original warning, which leaves the baseline prior unspecified. At the stated $50\%$, GPT-6 Sol, Gemma~4 31B, and Qwen3.5 9B with reasoning are close to the predictions obtained with prior weights. Opus~4.6 and Haiku~5.5 at medium effort fall below the Bayesian reference weight by $n=4$. Haiku~5.5 at low effort comes closest to the reference; its replies often contain no reasoning output at $n=1$.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the unspecified baseline prior.}
The original prompt states only that, if the description is wrong, ``the mean is unknown''. A Bayesian relevance update compares the marginal likelihoods of the demonstrations under the two component priors. The prompt therefore does not determine a unique posterior relevance probability.

For the $c=36$ description with a stated $50\%$ chance of irrelevance, the reference's fitted weights on the first $200$ tasks at $n=1$, $2$, $4$, $8$, and $16$ are $0.58$, $0.61$, $0.64$, $0.58$, and $0.54$ with the uniform baseline prior over $100$ to $999$. They are $0.49$, $0.49$, $0.53$, $0.49$, and $0.45$ with the actual task prior $\mathcal{N}(500,100^2)$; $0.52$, $0.54$, $0.58$, $0.53$, and $0.49$ with $\mathcal{N}(500,200^2)$; and $0.72$, $0.74$, $0.74$, $0.67$, and $0.60$ with a uniform baseline prior over $0$ to $2{,}000$. Averaging with prior weights gives $0.49$, $0.47$, $0.45$, $0.41$, and $0.35$.

At four demonstrations, the reference's fitted description weight exceeds that obtained with prior weights for every baseline prior examined. The model predictions and the comparison using prior weights are unchanged; only the Bayesian reference varies with the assumed baseline prior. Under the actual task prior, updating the relevance probability changes the fitted weight little before four demonstrations.

\paragraph{Stating the baseline prior.}
To remove this ambiguity, we rerun the stated-reliability condition on the same $200$ tasks, at $c=36$, stated irrelevance probabilities of $10\%$ and $50\%$, and $n=1$, $2$, and $4$. The prompt now states the reference's uniform baseline prior over $100$ to $999$. We use GPT-6 Sol at low effort and Haiku~5.5 at medium effort, with $1{,}200$ prompts each. Table~\ref{tab:fallback} compares their fitted description weights with those from the original runs.

At the stated $50\%$, the reference and prior-weighted predictions differ by at least $5$ on $123$ to $155$ tasks per cell. On these tasks, the share of replies closer to the reference than to the prior-weighted prediction rises from $0$--$5\%$ to $92$--$97\%$ for GPT-6 Sol, and from $21$--$41\%$ to $87$--$95\%$ for Haiku~5.5. At the stated $10\%$, at most $7$ tasks per cell separate the two rules by this amount, so the comparison is uninformative there.

A constant increase in the description weight could also make replies closer to the reference, because its posterior relevance probability exceeds the stated $50\%$ on most tasks. To test whether the weights vary with the evidence, we use each reply's \emph{normalized description weight}: its per-response weight divided by $c/(c+n)$, restricting to tasks with $|m-\bar y|\geq30$. For the scalar reference, this equals its posterior relevance probability $\pi_n$, up to the negligible boundary effects discussed above. For model predictions, the ratio can lie outside $[0,1]$ and does not identify an internal probability. Here $\pi_n$ ranges from $0.08$ to $0.88$ as the demonstrations provide different evidence for relevance; averaging with prior weights keeps the normalized weight constant.

We regress the models' normalized weights on $\pi_n$, pooling $n=1$, $2$, and $4$ at the stated $50\%$ ($269$ replies per model). With the baseline prior stated, the slopes are $0.96$ [$0.84$, $1.04$] for GPT-6 Sol and $0.98$ [$0.82$, $1.15$] for Haiku~5.5, compared with $0.01$ [$-0.09$, $0.11$] and $0.22$ [$-0.01$, $0.45$] under the original warning. The $95\%$ intervals resample the $153$ tasks. The corresponding intercepts with the prior stated are $0.01$ and $0.03$, and $R^2$ is $0.57$ and $0.33$.

The agreement also holds when the demonstrations count against the description. Among the $23$ replies with $\pi_n$ below $0.5$ (mean $0.33$), the mean normalized weights are $0.30$ and $0.38$ for the two models with the baseline prior stated, compared with $0.48$ and $0.50$ under the original warning. Both models therefore vary their description weights across tasks in approximately the amount predicted by Bayesian model averaging.

Summary ESS uses each model's no-description curve from the main run. At the stated $50\%$, averaging with prior weights gives $4.4$ and the Bayesian reference gives $5.7$. Stating the baseline prior raises ESS from $4.2$ to $5.4$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $3.8$ to $4.2$ for Haiku~5.5. At the stated $10\%$, it changes from $29.7$ to $25.0$ for GPT-6 Sol and from $32.6$ to $15.1$ for Haiku~5.5, compared with $27.5$ for the reference. The two models' fitted weights agree within $0.01$ in this condition (Table~\ref{tab:fallback}). Haiku's lower ESS therefore does not reflect lower average reliance on the description; it comes from a minority of replies far from the reference, some of which repeat the demonstration.

\subsection{Wording with Reasoning Enabled}

\paragraph{GPT-6 Sol.}
We evaluate the three alternative wordings at low effort for the three relevant descriptions and $n = 1$, $2$, and $4$, using the first $200$ tasks. Of $5{,}400$ replies, $5{,}399$ contain a number. With ``An earlier estimate put its mean at $m$, with a standard error of $\tau$'', every reply includes reasoning output, and the fitted description weight is within $0.01$ of the reference in every cell.

Under ``Its mean is about $m$, give or take $\tau$'' and ``Mean: $m \pm \tau$'', reasoning output is absent from $24$ to $51\%$ and $11$ to $50\%$ of replies per cell, respectively. Of those replies, $83\%$ and $87\%$ repeat the description value. At $c = 1$ and $n = 1$, the fitted description weights are consequently $0.89$ and $1.00$, compared with reference weight $0.50$. Among replies with reasoning output, $68\%$ and $43\%$ are within $1$ of the reference prediction, while $36\%$ and $68\%$ repeat the description value. These categories overlap at $c = 36$, where the description value and reference prediction can be close.

\paragraph{Haiku~5.5.}
We evaluate the same alternatives at medium effort; all $5{,}400$ replies contain a number. With ``An earlier estimate put its mean at $m$, with a standard error of $\tau$'', at least $71\%$ of replies in each cell include reasoning output; $99\%$ of these lie within $1$ of the reference prediction. Under ``Its mean is about $m$, give or take $\tau$'' and ``Mean: $m \pm \tau$'', reasoning output is absent from $4$ to $36\%$ and $2$ to $74\%$ of replies per cell, respectively. Even among replies with reasoning output, $71\%$ and $91\%$ repeat the description value, while $31\%$ and $20\%$ lie within $1$ of the reference prediction. At $c = 1$ and $n = 4$, its description weights are $0.72$ and $0.93$, compared with reference weight $0.20$.

All wordings provide a value $m$ and a scale $\tau$, but ``give or take'' and ``$\pm$'' do not identify $\tau$ as a prior standard deviation or specify a distribution for the mean. The comparison varies the explicitness of the probabilistic specification as well as its wording. The results show that accurate combination of descriptions and demonstrations under explicit statistical statements need not extend to these less explicit formulations.

\subsection{A Semantic Task}\label{app:semantic}

\paragraph{Task.}
A message has six binary properties: its sender is outside the organization; it requests a payment; it concerns travel; it requests computer access; it has a deadline within a day; and it contains personal contact information. Each message states every property as present or absent, using one of three phrasings for each value, such as ``I'm a contractor from another company.'' or ``I'm on the sales team here.'' Properties are sampled independently with probability one half, phrasings are sampled uniformly, and sentences appear in random order.

A policy uses exactly two properties and requires each to be present or absent. A message goes into folder A when both requirements hold, and into B otherwise. There are $\binom{6}{2}2^2=60$ such policies. The prompt describes this family and states that the company's policy was chosen uniformly from it. It then provides a policy note, $n$ previously filed messages with their folders, and four new messages. The model is asked for each new message's probability of going into A, as a number between $0$ and $1$, on four lines from ``1: \textless probability\textgreater'' to ``4: \textless probability\textgreater''. Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol identifies each property correctly in $97$--$100\%$ of $96$ messages; all three misreadings concern one phrasing, ``I'm not traveling for this.''

\paragraph{Policy notes.}
A broad note reads, for example, ``Policy note: whether it concerns travel is one of the two properties the policy uses,'' leaving $20$ possible policies. A specific note reads ``Policy note: to go into A, a message must request a payment. The other property the policy uses is whether it concerns travel,'' leaving $2$. These notes communicate constraints in ordinary language; the model must determine which policies satisfy them.

The unreliable note adds ``This note has a 50\% chance of being wrong, in which case it says nothing about the policy, which is then any of the 60, equally likely.'' In the generator, the note is relevant with probability one half and otherwise describes an independently drawn policy. The latter, irrelevant branch occurs in $92$ of the $200$ tasks. An independently generated note can agree with the actual policy by chance. Thus, \emph{relevance} denotes the latent generating branch, whereas \emph{compatibility} denotes whether a policy satisfies the note; the two are not interchangeable.

\paragraph{Reference and ESS.}
For a note asserted to be relevant, the reference predicts the fraction of policies consistent with the note and the filed messages that assign the new message to A. For an unreliable note, it averages this prediction with the prediction from policies consistent with the filed messages alone, weighting the two by the posterior relevance probability. The prior relevance probability is $0.5$; the posterior probability is obtained using the likelihood of the demonstrations under each branch. On $3{,}000$ simulated tasks, the reference's predictive probabilities are calibrated in every condition, and its posterior probability that the unreliable note is relevant matches the frequency of relevance: where it exceeds $0.7$, it averages $0.80$, and the note is relevant in $80\%$ of cases.

The loss is the squared error between the predicted probability of A and the true folder indicator, averaged over the four query messages. ESS follows Definition~\ref{def:ess}, using each learner's no-note curve at $n=0$, $2$, $4$, $6$, $8$, $12$, $16$, $20$, $24$, and $32$, with linear interpolation in $n$. On simulated tasks, this grid changes the reference ESS by at most $0.6$ relative to evaluation at every integer demonstration count.

\paragraph{Runs.}
We use $200$ tasks, each defined by a sampled policy, and $19$ conditions: no note at the ten demonstration counts above, and each note at $n=0$, $2$, and $4$. This gives $3{,}800$ prompts per configuration. Haiku~5.5 runs at medium effort; GPT-6 Sol runs without reasoning and at low effort. The command-line agents follow Appendix~\ref{app:llm}, with no tools, and no reply calls a tool. Fourteen replies from GPT-6 Sol at low effort contain no probability and are excluded; one Haiku~5.5 reply omits one of its four probabilities. Every Haiku~5.5 reply includes reasoning output, with a median of $3{,}008$ reasoning tokens. For GPT-6 Sol at low effort, $91\%$ of replies include reasoning output, with a median of $142$ reasoning tokens.

\paragraph{Results.}
Table~\ref{tab:semantic} reports each note's ESS. With reasoning, both models are within $2$ demonstrations of the reference in every condition except GPT-6 Sol's specific note at $n=4$: its ESS is $26.1$, compared with $14.8$ for the reference. Its loss with this note, $0.008$, is close to the reference's $0.007$, but its no-note curve flattens beyond $n=16$, with loss $0.022$ at $n=20$, compared with $0.004$ for the reference. It consequently needs more demonstrations on its own baseline curve to match the note's error reduction.

For the unreliable note, we compare predictions with the Bayesian reference and with a comparator that averages the two component predictions using their prior weights. This comparator retains the stated $50\%$ weight unless the filed messages rule out every policy compatible with the note; in that case, it assigns zero weight to the note-conditioned component. We use query messages on which the two predictions differ by at least $0.05$: about $150$ at $n=2$ and $200$ at $n=4$, out of $800$. The share of predictions closer to the reference is $90\%$ at $n=2$ and $99\%$ at $n=4$ for Haiku~5.5; $77\%$ and $87\%$ for GPT-6 Sol at low effort; and $45\%$ at both counts for GPT-6 Sol without reasoning.

Without reasoning, GPT-6 Sol learns slowly from demonstrations. Its loss without a note is still $0.078$ at $n=32$, compared with $0.000$ for the reference at the reported precision. Measured against this weaker baseline, a note can have an ESS as large as the reference's or larger. For example, the specific note has ESS $13.5$ at $n=2$, although the model's loss with that note is $0.130$, compared with $0.027$ for the reference. This illustrates why learner-specific ESS must be interpreted alongside absolute prediction error.

\section{Additional Related Work}\label{app:related}

\paragraph{Task descriptions and demonstrations.}
\citet{weller2020} formalize learning from task descriptions by distinguishing the latent task, its natural-language description, and individual input--output examples. We use \emph{task description} for information about the task supplied in the prompt, including a numeric description token in the meta-trained setting and probabilistic statements or policy constraints in the language-model experiments. A \emph{demonstration} is an observed input--output example; in scalar mean estimation, it is an observation at the fixed input $x=1$. Summaries distilled from demonstrations can serve as descriptions \citep{honda2025}; our controlled experiments instead specify the joint distribution of task, description, and demonstrations.

\paragraph{Prior information and uncertain relevance.}
The two-component construction is related to robust mixture priors, which combine informative and baseline distributions and update their component weights after observing data \citep{schmidli2014}. Our mixture is specified directly, rather than derived from a historical-data meta-analysis. Reliability $p$ is the prior probability of the description-conditioned component; $\pi_n$ is its posterior probability. Averaging component predictions using $\pi_n$ is Bayesian model averaging \citep{hoeting1999}. The experimental comparison that retains $p$ instead averages with prior weights. Fixed prior weights in a robust prior still yield updated posterior weights.

Our relevance terminology also has a conceptual precedent in information fusion, where relevance is distinguished from truthfulness \citep{pichon2012}. That work uses belief functions; our probability model is different. In our model, an irrelevant description is independent of the task and may happen to agree with it. Thus irrelevance is a generating condition, whereas prior--data conflict concerns disagreement between prior information and observed data. Related approaches discount external information according to predictive loss \citep{reznik2026} or accommodate conflict with heavy-tailed distributions \citep{ohagan2012}.

\paragraph{Specificity and description weights.}
Specificity takes a concrete form in each setting. In the isotropic Gaussian model, it is represented by prior precision $c$; a partial description also specifies the number $k$ of constrained directions. In the semantic experiment, broad and specific notes leave different numbers of compatible policies. These constructions do not define a common numerical scale across tasks. In Gaussian mean estimation, the reference combines the prior mean and sample mean with weights proportional to their precisions \citep{neuenschwander2020}. Fitted and per-response description weights summarize model predictions. Their normalized values can be compared with posterior component probabilities, but need not themselves be probabilities.

\paragraph{Effective sample size and predictive comparisons.}
Prior ESS has several definitions, including matching local information \citep{morita2008}, matching expected posterior loss \citep{reimherr2021}, and quantifying sample savings at a target estimation accuracy \citep{wiesenfarth2020}. Our definition follows the risk-matching approach but evaluates population prediction error and allows a non-Bayesian learner. Its dependence on the demonstration count is part of the definition. The word \emph{predictive} specifies what is matched; it does not assert the additive predictive-consistency property studied by \citet{neuenschwander2020}.

Two predictive comparisons are especially close. \citet{lescao2021} average horizontal data advantages over an accuracy range in supervised fine-tuning; we evaluate a pointwise difference in demonstration counts. \citet{selby2025} compare priors elicited from language models with data-based predictors by the observations needed to match predictive loss; we study descriptions supplied to a learner and how their benefit changes after demonstrations. Beyond ICL, an instruction can replace up to $100$ execution examples in the fine-tuning settings studied by \citet{cook2026}. \citet{wenliang2026} compare optimized prompts with demonstrations in terms of their length and effect.

\paragraph{Demonstrations, text, and probabilistic prediction.}
Other work examines semantic priors and flipped labels \citep{wei2023}, weak dependence on demonstration labels \citep{min2022}, Bayesian scaling laws \citep{arora2025}, and Bayesian reference curves for in-context function learning \citep{akata2026}. Text supplied alongside numbers can change numerical predictions \citep{requeima2024}. Related work studies how language-model outputs compare with Bayesian inference and how training can improve probabilistic reasoning \citep{qiu2026}; \citet{he2025} examine Bayesian consistency during iterative reasoning on open-ended questions. Our numerical experiments state uncertainty explicitly, allowing comparison with a specified Bayesian reference as precision and relevance probability vary. The semantic task extends this comparison to ordinary-language constraints on a finite family of classification policies.
